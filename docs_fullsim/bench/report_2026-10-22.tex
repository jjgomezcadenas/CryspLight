Figure~\ref{fig:rms} shows the rms of every channel in the three runs with HoldDelay 0: 12--13
ADC counts in the high gain, with 90\% of the channels within $\pm0.4$ counts of the median
at 25~ns and $\pm0.7$ at 50 and 87.5~ns, and about 5.8 counts in the low gain, the same on all
boards. The blind channels (Section~\ref{sec:sipm}) are in red.

Part of this noise is shared by all the channels of a chip, and it can be separated because the
32 channels of a Citiroc are read at the same trigger. The steps are shown below on one chip
that works normally (board 2, channels 32--63, run 26), high gain, and on all the chips where a
step needs them.

For channel $i$ in event $e$, $x_i(e)$ is the value read. The pedestal of the channel, $p_i$,
is the median of $x_i$ over the events of the run. The deviation from the pedestal in this
event is
\begin{equation*}
d_i(e) = x_i(e) - p_i .
\end{equation*}

\subsection{Outliers of a channel}\label{sec:outliers}

Outliers in the chip should not be taken into account for averages or common mode. To exclude
them we define the following procedure.

First, define the reference of the chip in event $e$, $r(e)$, as the median of the
deviations of its 32 channels in that event:
\begin{equation*}
r(e) = \mathrm{median}_{\,i}\big(d_i(e)\big) = \mathrm{median}_{\,i}\big(x_i(e) - p_i\big) ,
\end{equation*}
where the median is taken over the channels $i$, so that there is one value of $r$ per
event. The median is robust against outliers, that is, one or a few channels with values far
from the others cannot move it.

The MAD of a set of values $v_k$ is obtained in two steps:
\begin{enumerate}
\item compute $m$, the median of the $v_k$;
\item compute the MAD, $M$, as the median of the distances, $M = \mathrm{median}\big(|v_k - m|\big)$.
\end{enumerate}
By construction the MAD is robust against outliers.
%
For a Gaussian of width $\sigma$, the interval $\pm 0.674\,\sigma$ around the centre
contains half of the probability, and therefore the MAD of a Gaussian, $M_g$, is
$0.674\,\sigma$. Then $\sigma = M_g / 0.674 = 1.4826\, M_g$. Hence, if a set of values is
Gaussian and its MAD, $M$, is known, its width is $\sigma = 1.4826\, M$.

Now, for channel $i$, consider the set of values
\begin{equation*}
v_i^e = d_i(e) - r(e) ,
\end{equation*}
where $e$ runs over the events of the run. $v_i^e$ measures how far the channel strays from
the rest of its chip in each event. Following the definition of the MAD, $m_i$ is the median
of the $v_i^e$ over the events (close to zero, since both the pedestal and the reference of
the event have been subtracted), and $M_i = \mathrm{median}_{\,e}\big(|v_i^e - m_i|\big)$.
Assuming that the distribution of the $v_i^e$ is Gaussian, its width is
\begin{equation*}
s_i = 1.4826\, M_i .
\end{equation*}

Now we can define the pull of channel $i$ in event $e$ in terms of the robust estimator of the
noise of the channel $s_i$:
%
\begin{equation*}
z_i(e) = \frac{d_i(e) - r(e)}{s_i} .
\end{equation*}
%
To decide if a given channel is an outlier, we impose a cut on the pull, which comes in units
of the noise. We use $|z_i(e)| > 5$, but a tighter cut, such as 3, would serve as well; for
Gaussian noise an entry beyond $\pm5$ has a probability of $6\times10^{-7}$. The cut assumes a
symmetric noise and is applied in the runs with HoldDelay 0 (Section~\ref{sec:noiseresults}).

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{noise_steps_outliers.png}
\caption{Run 26 (HoldDelay 0): pull $z = (d_i - r)/s_i$ of every channel of every chip read,
in all the events except the jumps of the chip (Section~\ref{sec:jumps}), with the Gaussian of
unit width (blue) and the cut at $|z| = 5$ (red). The excess in the bin at zero comes from the
integer ADC values.}
\label{fig:outliers}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:outliers} shows the distribution of $z$ over all channels and events of
run 26. It follows the Gaussian over five orders of magnitude, with slightly longer tails:
7 entries of 2.6 million are beyond $\pm5$, against 1.5 expected. In all the runs with
HoldDelay 0 there are 41 outliers in about $10^7$ entries, spread over 34 channels with at most
two in one channel: no channel is faulty.

\subsection{Common mode}\label{sec:cmode}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{noise_steps_A.png}
\caption{Example chip (board 2, channels 32--63, run 26), data (black) against the same data
with each channel shuffled independently (orange). Top: mean of the 32 channels in the first
100 rows, and its distribution. Bottom: correlation between every pair of channels, in the data
(left) and after shuffling (right).}
\label{fig:stepA}
\end{figure}

Common mode occurs when all the channels of a chip move together in a given event. The effect, if not subtracted, adds to the rms of each channel. To visualize the effect consider two different ways of taking the mean of the 32 channels in a chip. 

\begin{enumerate}
\item Time-ordered mean ($m_t$), in which the mean is taken for the 32 channels for each event.
\item Time-shuffled mean ($m_s$), in which the values of each channel are put in a random order over the events, independently for each channel, before taking the mean.
\end{enumerate}

The top panels of Figure~\ref{fig:stepA} compare the two means on
an example chip. The left (top) panel shows the $m_t$ and $m_s$ as a function of event number and the right (top) panel shows the distribution of the means. The rms of $m_t$ is 6.2 counts, while the rms of $m_s$ is 2.1. 

Now, in the example chip, the rms of one channel is 11.9 counts (median of the channels of the chip, Figure~\ref{fig:rms}). If the channels are all independent
in the chip, the rms of the mean is expected to be $11.9/\sqrt{32}=2.1$, corresponding to $m_s$ (where the channels are independent by definition). Instead, the rms of $m_t$ is 6.2 counts, showing a correlated noise term for all the channels.
This is also clearly seen in the bottom plots of Figure~\ref{fig:stepA}, where the correlation between each pair of channels in the chip is shown before and after shuffling. The left (bottom) panel shows 
that in the data every pair of channels is correlated by about 0.25, uniformly over the chip,
and not correlated after shuffling.

In summary, the channels share a common fluctuation, which varies continuously from event to event. To quantify it we define
the common mode of an event as the time-ordered mean $m_t$ computed without the outliers, that
is, the mean of the deviations of the channels that are not outliers in that event:
\begin{equation*}
\mathrm{CM}(e) = \frac{1}{n(e)}\sum_{i \,\notin\, \text{outliers}} d_i(e) .
\end{equation*}
As for the outliers, the blind channels defined below, which do not receive the common mode,
are left out of this mean.

\centering

\caption{Common mode of every chip read in run 26 (high gain, 25~ns, HoldDelay 0, without the
jumps of board 0): rms of CM, the rms expected without common mode ($\sigma_\mathrm{tot}/\sqrt{n}$,

\label{tab:cm}
\end{table}

Table~\ref{tab:cm} gives the common mode of the eight chips read in run 26. In every chip the
rms of CM is 6.2--7.2 counts, about three times the 2.1--2.4 expected without common mode.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{noise_steps_C.png}
\caption{Left: deviation of channel 40 of the example chip against the common mode, one point
per event of run 26, with the fitted slope (blue) and slope 1 (red). Right: slopes $b_i$ of all
the channels of all chips in the runs with HoldDelay 0; below 0.75 the channels are blind.}
\label{fig:stepC}
\end{figure}

The separation of the coherent and incoherent noise (Section~\ref{sec:widths}) assumes that
every channel receives the same common mode. This can be checked channel by channel. If channel
$i$ takes a fraction $b_i$ of the common mode, its deviation in each event is
\begin{equation*}
d_i(e) = b_i\, \mathrm{CM}(e) + \epsilon_i(e) ,
\end{equation*}
with $\epsilon_i$ its own noise, independent of CM. Plotting $d_i(e)$ against
$\mathrm{CM}(e)$, we obtain a straight line of slope
$b_i$ (Figure~\ref{fig:stepC}, left), whose value can be found by least-squares fit:
\begin{equation*}
b_i = \frac{\mathrm{cov}(d_i, \mathrm{CM})}{\mathrm{var}(\mathrm{CM})} ,
\end{equation*}
with the covariance and the variance taken over the events. A channel that takes the full
common mode has $b_i = 1$; one that does not receive it, $b_i = 0$. The right panel of
Figure~\ref{fig:stepC} shows the slopes of all the channels of all chips. Those on the right
of the dashed line lie between 0.82 and 1.17, with median 1.00. The 15 channels on the left,
with slopes below 0.45, are blind channels (the electronics is alive but the channel
is not connected to its SiPM; Section~\ref{sec:sipm}).



\subsection{Jumps of the chip}\label{sec:jumps}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{noise_steps_B.png}
\caption{Run 26: common mode of every event along the run for the example chip (top) and for
board 0, channels 32--63 (bottom), with the cut (dashed) and the jumps (red circles).}
\label{fig:stepB}
\end{figure}

If the whole chip moves in an event, the common mode of that event falls out of its own
distribution. With $c$ the centre of that distribution over the run (its median) and
$\sigma_\mathrm{CM} = 1.4826\,\mathrm{MAD}(\mathrm{CM})$ its width, measured as $s_i$
(Section~\ref{sec:outliers}), an event is a jump if
\begin{equation*}
|\mathrm{CM}(e) - c| > 5\, \sigma_\mathrm{CM} ,
\end{equation*}
which a Gaussian common mode does with a probability of $6\times10^{-7}$ per event; jumps are
counted and set aside (F10). Figure~\ref{fig:stepB} shows the common mode of every event of
run 26. On the example chip it stays within $\pm29$ counts, inside the cut at $\pm31$: no
jump. On board 0 (channels 32--63), 45 events (0.5\%) are jumps, with a common mode of 38 to 270 counts in
absolute value, often in two consecutive triggers.

\subsection{Total, coherent and incoherent noise}\label{sec:widths}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{noise_steps_D.png}
\caption{Top: total, coherent and incoherent noise of every chip read in run 26 (HG 25~ns), with
$\sqrt{\sigma_\mathrm{coh}^2 + \sigma_\mathrm{inc}^2}$ as open circles. Bottom: $\sigma_\mathrm{tot}$
against $\sqrt{\sigma_\mathrm{coh}^2 + \sigma_\mathrm{inc}^2}$ for every chip in the runs with
HoldDelay 0.}
\label{fig:stepD}
\end{figure}

Write the deviation of channel $i$ in event $e$ as the sum of a part common to the chip and a
part of its own,
\begin{equation*}
d_i(e) = c(e) + \epsilon_i(e) ,
\end{equation*}
where $c$ is the same for the $N$ channels of the chip (32, fewer if some are blind) and the $\epsilon_i$ are independent
of $c$ and of each other. Call $\sigma_\mathrm{coh}$ the rms of $c$ (the coherent noise) and
$\sigma_\mathrm{inc}$ that of $\epsilon_i$ (the incoherent noise, assumed equal for all
channels). The rms of one channel, its total noise, is then
\begin{equation*}
\sigma_\mathrm{tot}^2 = \sigma_\mathrm{coh}^2 + \sigma_\mathrm{inc}^2 .
\end{equation*}

The common mode, the mean of the $N$ deviations, contains $c$ and the mean of the $N$
independent $\epsilon_j$, whose variance is $\sigma_\mathrm{inc}^2/N$:
\begin{equation*}
\mathrm{CM}(e) = c(e) + \frac{1}{N}\sum_j \epsilon_j(e) , \qquad
\mathrm{var}(\mathrm{CM}) = \sigma_\mathrm{coh}^2 + \frac{\sigma_\mathrm{inc}^2}{N} .
\end{equation*}
The common mode is therefore not exactly $c$: it keeps a small share of the incoherent noise.

Subtracting the common mode from a channel removes $c$, but also $1/N$ of its own
$\epsilon_i$, which is part of the mean:
\begin{equation*}
d_i(e) - \mathrm{CM}(e) = \Big(1 - \frac{1}{N}\Big)\epsilon_i(e) - \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j \ne i}\epsilon_j(e) ,
\qquad
\mathrm{var}(d_i - \mathrm{CM}) = \frac{N-1}{N}\,\sigma_\mathrm{inc}^2 .
\end{equation*}

The three quantities are measured as follows, taking for each the median over the channels of
the chip that are not blind, $N$ being their number:
\begin{enumerate}
\item the total noise $\sigma_\mathrm{tot}$ is the median of $\sigma_i$, the rms of $d_i$ over
  the events;
\item the incoherent noise follows from $\sigma'_i$, the rms of $d_i - \mathrm{CM}$:
  $\sigma_\mathrm{inc} = \sqrt{N/(N-1)}\;\mathrm{median}(\sigma'_i)$;
\item the coherent noise follows from the variance of the common mode:
  $\sigma_\mathrm{coh}^2 = \mathrm{var}(\mathrm{CM}) - \sigma_\mathrm{inc}^2/N$.
\end{enumerate}
The relation $\sigma_\mathrm{tot}^2 = \sigma_\mathrm{coh}^2 + \sigma_\mathrm{inc}^2$ is then a
check of the three measurements. On the example chip $\sigma_\mathrm{tot} = 11.9$,
$\sigma_\mathrm{coh} = 5.9$ and $\sigma_\mathrm{inc} = 10.4$ counts; the check
$\sigma_\mathrm{tot}^2 = \sigma_\mathrm{coh}^2 + \sigma_\mathrm{inc}^2$ holds within 0.1\%.
Figure~\ref{fig:stepD} shows the three for every chip in run 26, and the check for every chip in
the runs with HoldDelay 0, where it holds within 0.8\%.

\subsection{Correlation of the common mode between chips}\label{sec:board}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{noise_steps_E.png}
\caption{Run 26: correlation coefficient of the common modes of every pair of chips read,
labelled by board and channels.}
\label{fig:stepE}
\end{figure}

The origin of the common mode can be further explored by 
comparing chips. For two chips, the
correlation coefficient of their common modes is computed over the events read by both, matched
by trigger. Notice that a source of noise inside the Citiroc ---for example, the reference voltages of each chip--- would result
in uncorrelated common modes per chip. Instead, a source  on
the board ---the usual suspect here is the HV module that biases the SiPMs--- would correlate the
two chips of the board.  A source of noise common to the whole system, such as a pick-up from the power line,
would correlate all the chips.

Figure~\ref{fig:stepE} shows the correlation for every pair of chips in run 26. The two chips
of the same board are correlated by 0.58--0.66, and chips of different boards by less than
0.12. This implies that the common mode is a per-board effect and there is no source common to the system. The
correlation within a board grows with the shaping time, to 0.74--0.77 at 50~ns and
0.81--0.85 at 87.5~ns, as the coherent noise itself does (Section~\ref{sec:noiseresults}).

\section{Blind channels}\label{sec:sipm}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{staircase_11.png}
\caption{Staircase of 21 October (scan 11, board 0): rate of the timing discriminator against
its coarse threshold, one curve per channel, with the T-OR in black.}
\label{fig:stair}
\end{figure}

The staircases taken on 21 October on board 0, with the SiPMs above breakdown, scan the
threshold of the timing discriminator of each channel and count its rate
(Figure~\ref{fig:stair}). Above the noise plateau, which ends at 180--185 DAC in all
channels, the rate falls slowly with the steps of the dark-count spectrum (1, 2, 3 \ldots\ pe)
in 54 channels. Six channels, \textbf{15, 55, 57, 59, 61 and 63}, fall from the plateau to zero
within 10 DAC and count 0--4~Hz 30 DAC above the edge, against a median of $6\times10^5$~Hz:
they see no avalanches (F4). Since the SiPMs are connected, the signal is lost between SiPM and
Citiroc, in a bad contact or in the connector. Four more, \textbf{0, 2, 8 and 10}, count 7--10
times less than the median (F5).

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{bench_slopes.png}
\caption{Slope of each channel against the common mode of its chip, median over the runs with
HoldDelay 0 (bars from the lowest to the highest run), with the cut at 0.75. In red, the six
channels of board 0 silent in the staircases of 21 October.}
\label{fig:slopes}
\end{figure}

The pedestal runs, taken below breakdown, find the same channels without staircases. In the
rms they show as quieter channels (red in Figure~\ref{fig:rms}), mostly because they lack the
common mode: at 87.5~ns they sit at 0.81--0.90 of the median rms of their board, but the
quietest normal channel is at 0.90, so the rms does not separate them cleanly. The common mode
does, because it enters through the SiPM: the six channels take less than 0.45 of it, every
channel of board 0 with avalanches more than 0.82 (Figure~\ref{fig:slopes}). The same
criterion, a slope below 0.75, finds nine channels on the other boards:
\begin{itemize}
\item board 1: 61 and 63;
\item board 3: 45, 47, 49 and 60;
\item board 4: 59, 61 and 63;
\end{itemize}
and none on board 2. These are predictions for the staircases of the five boards (menu 2).
As on board 0, the odd channels 55--63 recur, on boards 1 and 4, which points to the adapter
boards or the connectors. The weak channels of F5 have normal slopes, as expected: they see
their SiPM.

\section{Issues}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Half of boards 1 and 4 reads zero (F7).} Channels 0--31 of boards 1 and 4 give 0
  in every event, in both gains and all nine runs; channels 32--63 of the same boards are
  normal. 
%  The configuration stored in the files is the same for these two chips as for the
%  others: readout, discriminators and preamplifiers enabled, the same gains, thresholds and
%  shaping, and per-channel pedestal calibrations of 102--271 counts like those of the chips
%  that work. \texttt{Pedestal} = 0 is not the cause either, since the live channels have
%  essentially no value at zero. The same chip failing on two boards with a normal
%  configuration points to the readout, presumably firmware or DAQ. 64 of the 320 channels
%  have therefore no data. \textbf{JMB}: needs a fix in the readout; reading board 1 with
%  Janus, CAEN's software, would tell whether the problem is in the DAQ.
\item \textbf{Common-mode jumps of board 0 (F10).} In 0.1--0.5\% of the events of runs 26--32,
  all the channels of board 0, on both chips, move together by 38--270 counts, up or down,
  often in two consecutive triggers (1~ms apart). This is what made board 0 look noisier in
  run 26.
%   its rms is 15.9 counts, but its robust width is 11.9, the same as on the other
%  boards. Runs 33 and 34 have no such events, and the other boards almost none (board 3, one
%  event in three runs; board 2, one in run 33). A shift of the whole board points either to its reference, supply or
%  ground, or to the readout. \textbf{JMB}: is it hardware (power, ground, cabling of board 0)
%  or firmware?
\item \textbf{Firmware (F9).} Board 2 runs firmware 0xa7070800, the others 0xa1230708. No
  effect is visible in the data: board 2 reads normally. \textbf{JMB}: is the difference
  intended, and could the firmware be behind F7 or F10?
\end{itemize}
