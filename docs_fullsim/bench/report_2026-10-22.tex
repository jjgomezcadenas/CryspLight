Figure~\ref{fig:rms} shows the rms of every channel in the three runs with HoldDelay 0: 12--13
ADC counts in the high gain, with 90\% of the channels within $\pm0.4$ counts of the median
at 25~ns and $\pm0.7$ at 50 and 87.5~ns, and about 5.8 counts in the low gain, the same on all

Part of this noise is shared by all the channels of a chip, and it can be separated because the
32 channels of a Citiroc are read at the same trigger. The steps are shown below on one chip
that works normally (board 2, channels 32--63, run 26), high gain.

\subsection{Deviations from the pedestal}\label{sec:dev}

For channel $i$ in event $e$, with $x_i(e)$ the value read,
\begin{equation*}
d_i(e) = x_i(e) - \operatorname{median}_e\, x_i .
\end{equation*}

\subsection{Outliers of a channel}\label{sec:outliers}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{noise_steps_outliers.png}
\caption{Pull $z = (d_i - r)/s_i$ of every channel of every chip read, in all the events that
are not jumps, at HoldDelay 0 (left, run 26) and 496~ns (right, run 33), with the Gaussian of
unit width (blue) and the cut at $|z| = 5$ (red). The excess in the bin at zero and the comb
on the right come from the integer ADC values.}
\label{fig:outliers}
\end{figure}

A single channel may depart from the rest of its chip in one event, a fault of that channel
rather than noise, and it must not be taken for common mode. In each event the reference of the
chip is the median of the 32 deviations, $r(e) = \operatorname{median}_i d_i(e)$, which one
channel cannot move. The pull of channel $i$ in event $e$ is its distance from that reference
in units of its own noise,
\begin{equation*}
z_i(e) = \frac{d_i(e) - r(e)}{s_i} ,
\end{equation*}
where $s_i$ is measured robustly as 1.4826 times the median absolute deviation of $d_i - r$
over the events. The channel is an outlier in that event if $|z_i(e)| > 5$; outliers are left
out of what follows and counted for each channel. For Gaussian noise $z$ follows a Gaussian of
unit width, and an entry beyond $\pm5$ has a probability of $6\times10^{-7}$.

Figure~\ref{fig:outliers} shows the distribution of $z$ over all channels and events. At
HoldDelay 0 it follows the Gaussian over five orders of magnitude, with slightly longer tails:
in run 26, 7 entries of 2.6 million are beyond $\pm5$, against 1.5 expected; in all the runs with HoldDelay 0 there are 41 outliers in about $10^7$ entries, spread over 34
channels with at most two in one channel, so no channel is faulty. With a long window the
distribution is asymmetric: the stored maximum has a longer tail upwards (skewness 0.76 at
496~ns), which crosses $+5$, and the outliers found there, all positive, are that tail rather
than faults. The criterion assumes a symmetric noise and is meaningful at HoldDelay 0; at long
windows leaving those entries out changes the widths by less than 0.1 counts.

\subsection{Common mode}\label{sec:cmode}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{noise_steps_A.png}
\caption{Example chip (board 2, channels 32--63, run 26), data (black) against the same data
with each channel shuffled independently (orange). Top: mean of the 32 channels in the first
100 rows, and its distribution. Bottom: correlation between every pair of channels, in the data
(left) and after shuffling (right).}
\label{fig:stepA}
\end{figure}

The common mode of an event is the mean of the deviations of the channels that are not
outliers (32 in almost every event),
\begin{equation*}
\mathrm{CM}(e) = \frac{1}{n(e)}\sum_{i \,\notin\, \text{outliers}} d_i(e) .
\end{equation*}
If the 32 channels were independent, CM would still vary from event to event, only less: its
rms would be that of one channel divided by $\sqrt{32}$. To see whether there is a common mode,
the measured CM is compared with a reference made from the data themselves. The table of the
deviations has one row per event and one column per channel; shuffling puts the values of each
column in an independent random order. Each channel keeps its values, and so its pedestal, its
rms and the shape of its distribution, but the 32 values in a row no longer come from the same
trigger, so nothing common can move them together. Figure~\ref{fig:stepA} compares the two on
the example chip:
\begin{itemize}
\item the mean of the 32 channels has an rms of 6.2 counts in the data and 2.1 after
  shuffling, which is the rms of one channel, 11.9 counts, divided by $\sqrt{32}$;
\item in the data every pair of channels is correlated by about 0.25, uniformly over the chip,
  and after shuffling by zero.
\end{itemize}
The channels share a common fluctuation, which varies continuously from event to event. A
uniform correlation of 0.25 is what a common part with a quarter of the variance of each
channel gives, and it agrees with the separation of Section~\ref{sec:widths}
($\sigma_\mathrm{coh}^2/\sigma_\mathrm{tot}^2 = 5.9^2/11.9^2 = 0.25$).

\subsection{Coupling of each channel}\label{sec:coupling}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{noise_steps_C.png}
\caption{Left: common mode of the example chip. Centre: deviation of channel 40 against the
common mode, with the fitted slope. Right: slopes $b_i$ of all channels in the runs with
HoldDelay 0; the channels below 0.75 do not see their SiPM.}
\label{fig:stepC}
\end{figure}

The separation assumes that every channel receives the same common mode. This is checked with
the slope of each channel against it,
\begin{equation*}
b_i = \frac{\mathrm{cov}_e(d_i, \mathrm{CM})}{\mathrm{var}_e\, \mathrm{CM}} ,
\end{equation*}
which is 1 for a channel that takes the full common mode (Figure~\ref{fig:stepC}). It is 0.96
for the channel of the middle panel, and between 0.85 and 1.2 (median 1.04) for all the
channels of all chips but 15, which take much less (right panel). These 15 channels are those
whose SiPM is not seen (Section~\ref{sec:sipm}).

\subsection{Jumps of the chip}\label{sec:jumps}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{noise_steps_B.png}
\caption{Run 26. Left: common mode of every event along the run for the example chip (top)
and for board 0, channels 32--63 (bottom), with the cut (dashed) and the jumps (red circles).
Right: deviations of the channels of board 0, channels 32--63, around a jump (colour saturated
at $\pm40$ counts).}
\label{fig:stepB}
\end{figure}

If the whole chip moves in an event, the common mode of that event falls out of its own
distribution. With $\sigma_\mathrm{CM}$ the robust width of the common mode over the run (1.4826
times its median absolute deviation), an event is a jump if
\begin{equation*}
|\mathrm{CM}(e) - \operatorname{median}_e \mathrm{CM}| > 6\, \sigma_\mathrm{CM} ,
\end{equation*}
which a Gaussian common mode does with a probability of $2\times10^{-9}$ per event; jumps are
counted and set aside (F10). Figure~\ref{fig:stepB} shows the common mode of every event of
run 26. On the example chip it stays within $\pm29$ counts, inside the cut at $\pm37$: no
jump. On board 0, 44 events (0.4\%) are jumps, with a common mode of 42 to 230 counts in
absolute value, often in two consecutive triggers; the map of the deviations around one of
them shows the whole chip moving together.

\subsection{Total, coherent and incoherent noise}\label{sec:widths}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.55\textwidth]{noise_steps_D.png}
\caption{Example chip: deviations of all channels in all events, before and after subtracting
the common mode of each event.}
\label{fig:stepD}
\end{figure}

\begin{align*}
\sigma_\mathrm{tot} &= \operatorname{median}_i\, \mathrm{rms}_e\, d_i , \\
\sigma_\mathrm{inc} &= \operatorname{median}_i\, \mathrm{rms}_e \big(d_i - \mathrm{CM}\big)\,\sqrt{32/31} , \\
\sigma_\mathrm{coh}^2 &= \mathrm{var}_e\, \mathrm{CM} - \sigma_\mathrm{inc}^2/32 .
\end{align*}
The two corrections come from the mean itself: subtracting the mean removes from each channel
1/32 of its own noise, and the mean keeps $1/\sqrt{32}$ of the incoherent noise of the
channels, which is not common. On the example chip $\sigma_\mathrm{tot} = 11.9$,
$\sigma_\mathrm{coh} = 5.9$ and $\sigma_\mathrm{inc} = 10.4$ counts; the check
$\sigma_\mathrm{tot}^2 = \sigma_\mathrm{coh}^2 + \sigma_\mathrm{inc}^2$ holds within 0.1\%.
Figure~\ref{fig:stepD} shows the narrowing of the distribution when the common mode is
subtracted.

\subsection{Results}\label{sec:noiseresults}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{noise_vs_shaping.png}
\caption{Runs 26--28: total, coherent and incoherent noise of the high gain against the
shaping time (median over the eight chips read, bars from the lowest to the highest chip),
and the low gain at 87.5~ns. Left, in ADC counts, with the model scaled to the incoherent
noise at 87.5~ns; right, divided by the value at 87.5~ns.}
\label{fig:shaping}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{noise_vs_holddelay.png}
\caption{Runs 26 and 29--33, high gain at 25~ns: rise of the pedestal from HoldDelay 0 in
units of its rms (left, median over the channels of each board) and rms relative to HoldDelay
0 (right), with the model (maximum of white noise shaped at 25~ns over the window).}
\label{fig:hold}
\end{figure}

Applied to every chip of every run (Figures~\ref{fig:shaping} and~\ref{fig:hold}):
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The noise barely depends on the shaping.} The simulation assumes white series
  noise, which would be 1.87 times larger at 25~ns than at 87.5~ns. The total noise is instead
  0.94 times as large; the incoherent part grows only by 1.13, and the coherent part falls
  (0.72).
\item \textbf{A quarter to 40\% of the variance is coherent} (5.9 counts at 25~ns, 8.2 at
  87.5~ns). In the sum of the 64 channels, which gives the energy, it adds linearly and
  dominates: about 350 counts against 85 from the incoherent part at 25~ns.
\item \textbf{The common mode enters through the SiPM.} The channels whose SiPM is not
  connected do not receive it (Section~\ref{sec:coupling}). This points to the bias line of
  the SiPMs, the HV of each board, coupled to the inputs through the capacitance of the SiPMs,
  and it explains why the common mode is shared by the two chips of a board and not between
  boards.
\item \textbf{The pedestal rises slowly with the window.} With a longer HoldDelay the pedestal
  rises and narrows, as expected for the peak stretch, but at 296~ns it has risen by 0.5--0.6
  rms where the model predicts 2.2, and it is still rising at 496~ns.
\item \textbf{The low gain} has 0.44 times the noise of the high gain at the same shaping,
  where the model assumes the same noise in counts.
\end{itemize}
\noindent The comparison with the model of the simulation, the hypotheses and the measurements
that would test them are in the note \emph{Noise of the Citiroc front end: how it is measured,
and what the bench shows}.

\section{Channels that do not see their SiPM}\label{sec:sipm}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{staircase_11.png}
\caption{Staircase of 21 October (scan 11, board 0): rate of the timing discriminator against
its coarse threshold, one curve per channel, with the T-OR in black.}
\label{fig:stair}
\end{figure}

The staircases taken on 21 October on board 0, with the SiPMs above breakdown, scan the
threshold of the timing discriminator of each channel and count its rate
(Figure~\ref{fig:stair}). Above the noise plateau, which ends at 180--185 DAC in all
channels, the rate falls slowly with the steps of the dark-count spectrum (1, 2, 3 \ldots\ pe)
in 54 channels. Six channels, \textbf{15, 55, 57, 59, 61 and 63}, fall from the plateau to zero
within 10 DAC and count 0--4~Hz 30 DAC above the edge, against a median of $6\times10^5$~Hz:
they see no avalanches (F4). Since the SiPMs are connected, the signal is lost between SiPM and
Citiroc, in a bad contact or in the connector. Four more, \textbf{0, 2, 8 and 10}, count 7--10
times less than the median (F5).

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{bench_slopes.png}
\caption{Slope of each channel against the common mode of its chip, median over the runs with
HoldDelay 0 (bars from the lowest to the highest run), with the cut at 0.75. In red, the six
channels of board 0 silent in the staircases of 21 October.}
\label{fig:slopes}
\end{figure}

The pedestal runs, taken below breakdown, find the same channels without staircases. In the
rms they show as quieter channels (red in Figure~\ref{fig:rms}), mostly because they lack the
common mode: at 87.5~ns they sit at 0.81--0.90 of the median rms of their board, but the
quietest normal channel is at 0.90, so the rms does not separate them cleanly. The common mode
does, because it enters through the SiPM: the six channels take 0.02--0.6 of it, every
channel of board 0 with avalanches more than 0.85 (Figure~\ref{fig:slopes}). The same
criterion, a slope below 0.75, finds nine channels on the other boards:
\begin{itemize}
\item board 1: 61 and 63;
\item board 3: 45, 47, 49 and 60;
\item board 4: 59, 61 and 63;
\end{itemize}
and none on board 2. These are predictions for the staircases of the five boards (menu 2).
As on board 0, the odd channels 55--63 recur, on boards 1 and 4, which points to the adapter
boards or the connectors. The weak channels of F5 have normal slopes, as expected: they see
their SiPM.

\section{Two pedestal peaks in the plots grouped by node}\label{sec:node}

In the quick-look plots of these runs made at the bench, most channels of ``board 0'' and half
of the channels of ``board 1'' show two pedestal peaks, half of ``board 2'' shows nothing, and
only three boards appear. The plots group the measurements by the node number alone. The five
boards sit on two chains, boards 0 and 1 on chain 0 (nodes 0 and 1) and boards 2, 3 and 4 on
chain 1 (nodes 0, 1 and 2), so that grouping by node gives three groups and mixes boards:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Node 0} is boards 0 and 2. Each channel shows the pedestals of two different
  boards, hence two peaks, or one when the two pedestals happen to coincide.
\item \textbf{Node 1} is boards 1 and 3. In channels 0--31 board 1 reads 0 (F7) and a cut
  above 20 counts removes it, leaving board 3 alone; in channels 32--63 both boards are
  present and there are two peaks.
\item \textbf{Node 2} is board 4 alone, whose channels 0--31 read 0 (F7).
\end{itemize}

\begin{verbatim}

board  = [boards[(c, n)] for c, n in zip(obs["chain"], obs["node"])]
\end{verbatim}
and the spectra are then made per board and channel. \textbf{Laura}: with this change the

\section{Problems}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Half of boards 1 and 4 reads zero (F7).} Channels 0--31 of boards 1 and 4 give 0
  in every event, in both gains and all nine runs; channels 32--63 of the same boards are
  normal. The configuration stored in the files is the same for these two chips as for the
  others: readout, discriminators and preamplifiers enabled, the same gains, thresholds and
  shaping, and per-channel pedestal calibrations of 102--271 counts like those of the chips
  that work. \texttt{Pedestal} = 0 is not the cause either, since the live channels have
  essentially no value at zero. The same chip failing on two boards with a normal
  configuration points to the readout, presumably firmware or DAQ. 64 of the 320 channels
  have therefore no data. \textbf{JMB}: needs a fix in the readout; reading board 1 with
  Janus, CAEN's software, would tell whether the problem is in the DAQ.
\item \textbf{Common-mode jumps of board 0 (F10).} In 0.1--0.5\% of the events of runs 26--32,
  all the channels of board 0, on both chips, move together by 40--230 counts, up or down,
  often in two consecutive triggers (1~ms apart). This is what made board 0 look noisier in
  run 26: its rms is 15.9 counts, but its robust width is 11.9, the same as on the other
  boards. Runs 33 and 34 have no such events, and the other boards have none (board 3, one
  event in three runs). A shift of the whole board points either to its reference, supply or
  ground, or to the readout. \textbf{JMB}: is it hardware (power, ground, cabling of board 0)
  or firmware?
\item \textbf{Firmware (F9).} Board 2 runs firmware 0xa7070800, the others 0xa1230708. No
  effect is visible in the data: board 2 reads normally. \textbf{JMB}: is the difference
  intended, and could the firmware be behind F7 or F10?
\end{itemize}

\section{Summary}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The noise has a coherent part, shared by the 64 channels of a board and entering
  through the SiPMs.} Separating the common mode of each chip (Section~\ref{sec:noise}) gives
  25--40\% of the variance in it, correlated between the two chips of a board and not between
  boards. In the sum of the channels, which gives the energy, it dominates.
\item \textbf{The channels that do not see their SiPM are found in the pedestal runs}, because
  they do not receive the common mode: the six channels of board 0 silent in the staircases,
  and nine more predicted on boards 1, 3 and 4, to be checked with the staircases of menu 2
  (Section~\ref{sec:sipm}).
\item \textbf{The noise does not follow the model of the simulation.} It barely depends on the
  shaping, the pedestal rises slowly with the window, and the low gain has 0.44 times the
  noise of the high gain. The comparison and the hypotheses are in the note on the noise.
\item \textbf{The two pedestal peaks of the bench plots come from grouping the boards by node}
  (Section~\ref{sec:node}); each board has one pedestal per channel and all five are in the
  files.
\item \textbf{Problems for JMB:} channels 0--31 of boards 1 and 4 are not read (F7), board 0
  jumps as a whole in a few events per thousand (F10), and board 2 runs a different firmware
  (F9).
\end{enumerate}
