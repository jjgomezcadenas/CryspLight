\begin{abstract}
The CRYSP concept proposes the use of large monolithic crystals read out by large SiPMs connected to custom electronics. Two candidates are being evaluated, BGO and CsI ---or CsI(Tl)---. The crystals can be operated at room temperature or cooled in order to increase their light yield, but cooler temperatures imply a longer scintillation decay time. Cold operation also reduces the dark current, which improves performance but has an impact on the SPE calibration. The large SiPMs ($6 \times 6$ mm$^2$ by default) allow to reduce the number of electronic channels typically by a factor four w.r.t. conventional pixelated detectors, in exchange for a larger capacitance which increases parasitic noise and complicates calibration. 

Three readout systems have been studied: a waveform digitizer (CAEN DT2745), and two ASICs, namely 
the Citiroc~1A and the Radioroc2, both manufactured from CAEN. The digitizer is needed in real measurements
to establish the scintillation lifetime. The Citiroc~1A offers less flexibility than the  Radioroc2, but it is widely available, unlike the newer model. 

The room-temperature performance of BGO and CsI(Tl) is of interest and require the simulation of the two crystals with the three readouts. Cold operation of BGO at 250~K has the merit of increasing the light yield by a factor 1.7 in exchange for multiplying the lifetime by a factor 2.8. Cold operation of pure CsI at 77~K is also important. It multiplies by two the light yield of CsI(Tl) and offers a better lifetime (a single exponential of about 1 $\mu$s, while CsI(Tl) has a long-term exponential and afterglow which complicate its operation). 


\end{abstract}

\section{The problem}
\label{sec:purpose}

The baseline concept for CRYSP contemplates large monolithic scintillating crystals
($48 \times 48$~mm$^2$ in cross section and two radiation
lengths thick) read out by a $8 \times 8$ array of large SiPMs ($6 \times 6$~mm$^2$).
The crystals are polished, wrapped in Teflon and coupled through optical grease to the sensors.
The default SiPM is the S14160-6050HS from Hamamatsu.

A SiPM of $6 \times 6$~mm$^2$ has a
capacitance of about 2~nF, which slows its pulse and raises the noise of the
amplifier that reads it. The typical dark rate is several MHz. A good modeling of the sensor 
and the readout electronics is essential to understand the performance of the crystals. 

CRYSP considers as potential materials BGO and CsI (or CsI(Tl)). BGO is denser and has a larger photoelectric cross section, but its light yield at room temperature is modest, about 8000 photons/MeV. This light yield can be increased by a factor 1.7 by cooling the crystal to 250~K.

The performance is studied along three axes. The first is the material of
the crystal: BGO, dense and with a modest light yield, and \csitl, which
gives about seven times more light with a slower decay. The second is the
readout. A CAEN DT2745 digitizer records the waveform of every channel and
integrates the whole pulse; two Citiroc~1A chips of 32 channels each, and a
single Radioroc2 of 64 channels, shape the signal of every channel and store
one value of it per event. The third is the temperature. At room
temperature the light yield of BGO is low and the dark counts are high;
cooling raises the yield and lengthens the decay, and lowers the dark counts
by a factor of 2 every 9~K. One cold configuration is studied for now, BGO at
250~K, read out by the digitizer and by the Radioroc2. The Citiroc is left
out of it: its shaping, at most 87.5~ns, keeps only 12\% of the light of a
0.84~\us decay, so that with 1.7 times more light it would store 28\% less
than at room temperature (Section~\ref{sec:citiroc}). Table~\ref{tab:configs} gives the
three configurations of crystal and temperature.

The performance of the crystals is expressed in terms of the
efficiency with which the scintillation photons are turned into
photoelectrons, the number of photoelectrons per keV, the energy resolution
and the spatial resolution. The simulation starts from an optical Monte
Carlo of the light transport in the crystal and adds a model of the SiPM,
with its gain, crosstalk, afterpulses, dark counts and pulse shape, and a
model of each readout, so that its output is the charge per channel that
each readout would record. The document describes these models, the
quantities extracted from the simulated output, the tests that validate the
code and the origin of every parameter.

The performance is expressed by four quantities. The light efficiency is
the fraction of the scintillation photons that become photoelectrons in the
SiPMs, and with the light yield it gives the absolute response, the number
of photoelectrons per keV deposited. These two are properties of the crystal,
of the optical coupling and of the SiPMs, and do not depend on the readout.
What a readout records is a charge per channel, which contains more than
these photoelectrons ---the crosstalk and the afterpulses that each of them
causes, and the dark counts--- and, for the two ASICs, only the fraction of
the scintillation pulse that their shaping keeps. The photoelectron budget
of each readout therefore goes from the photons emitted to the charge
recorded, and back from the calibrated charge to the number of
photoelectrons, once the crosstalk, the afterpulses, the dark counts and the
stored fraction are taken out; that number, and not the number of fired
cells, is the absolute response measured by the readout. The energy
resolution is the width of the 511~keV photopeak in the sum of the 64
channels, and of the 1275~keV one. The spatial resolution is the precision
with which the position of the interaction is reconstructed from the 64
charges of the event.

To quantify these four quantities the simulation needs three models. The
first is a realistic model of the propagation of the light inside the
crystal: the emission of the scintillation photons, with their spectrum and
their decay, their reflection on the wrapped and polished faces, their
absorption, and their passage through the grease and the window of the SiPMs
onto the active area of a channel, with the gaps between the channels. The
second is a realistic model of the SiPM: its detection efficiency as a
function of the wavelength and of the overvoltage, its gain, its crosstalk
and afterpulses, its dark counts, and the pulse that an avalanche produces
at the input of the electronics, which the capacitance of the device shapes.
The third is a realistic model of each readout, from the input of the
amplifier to the value stored per channel: shaping, noise, trigger, peak
detection, conversion and saturation for the ASICs; sampling, noise,
trigger, gate and baseline for the digitizer. This document describes these
three models and only them.

The optical part of the simulation already existed: CryspLight, an optical
Monte Carlo code in Julia designed to run on a GPU, transports the
scintillation photons in a wrapped crystal read by an array of SiPMs. Its
initial implementation assumed an idealised readout, in which the SiPM array
covers the whole face without gaps and the light is monochromatic and
detected with a single efficiency. The simulation described here replaces
that ideal face by the actual array, so that a photon may fall between two
SiPMs, and lets it reach the active surface through the grease and the
window of the device. Each photon is given a wavelength, because the
efficiency with which a SiPM detects it depends on it. Each SiPM becomes a
device that turns its photoelectrons into an electrical signal, with its
gain, its crosstalk and its dark counts. And the electronics that measures
that signal is simulated, so that the result is the value each readout
records. How these extensions are added to the existing code is described in
Section~\ref{sec:implementation}.

\subsection{The three stages of the simulation}

The simulation runs in three stages, each one feeding the next through a
file.

\begin{enumerate}
\item {\bf Optical simulation.}
\begin{itemize}
\item It generates the gammas from the source, follows them to their energy
  deposits in the crystal, emits the scintillation photons and transports
  them on the GPU until they are absorbed or reach a SiPM.
\item It writes one file per run with, for every event, the energy and
  position of each interaction of the gamma in the crystal, the number of
  scintillation photons emitted and the number detected, and one record for
  every detected photoelectron: the channel that detected it, the time of
  detection and the wavelength of the photon.
\end{itemize}
\item {\bf SiPM and electronics simulation.}
\begin{itemize}
\item It reads the photoelectron records and applies the response of the
  SiPM, which turns every photoelectron into a current pulse of a given
  shape, multiplied by the gain and accompanied by crosstalk and
  afterpulsing, and adds the dark counts of the device.
\item It then applies the chain of the readout. For the two ASICs the pulses
  are amplified and shaped, compared with the discriminator thresholds,
  their maximum is stored by the peak detector until the hold, and the
  stored value is converted to ADC counts. For the digitizer the pulses are
  compared with the trigger threshold and integrated in the gate and the
  baseline window, with the noise of the sampled signal, which gives
  directly the charge that the analysis of the waveform would produce
  (Section~\ref{sec:digitizer}).
\item It writes one file per run with, for every event and channel, the
  charge in ADC counts on each path of the readout and the discriminator
  flag, in the format of Section~\ref{sec:formats}, together with the
  energy deposits and the photoelectron counts of the first stage.
\end{itemize}
\item {\bf Analysis.}
\begin{itemize}
\item It reads that file and extracts from it the performance: the gain of
  each channel from its own dark-count spectrum, its pedestal and noise,
  the photoelectron budget, the energy spectrum of the sum of the channels
  and its resolution, and the maps of the response over the array
  (Section~\ref{sec:comparison}).
\end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

Writing a file between the stages separates the slow part of the
simulation from the fast one. The optical transport takes minutes on the GPU
for a run of $10^5$ events; the second and third stages take seconds to
minutes. A change in the parameters of the SiPMs or of the electronics, or
the choice of another readout, therefore requires rerunning only the last
two stages, and the photoelectron records are reused.

\section{Geometry}
\label{sec:geometry}

\subsection{Crystal and wrapping}

The crystal is a rectangular box, $48 \times 48$~mm$^2$ in cross section and
22.4~mm (BGO) or 37.2~mm (\csitl) thick, polished on its six faces. Five faces
are wrapped by hand in several layers of PTFE tape. The tape does not touch
the crystal everywhere, so the model places a thin layer of air between the
two. A photon that reaches a wrapped face is first reflected or transmitted at
the crystal-to-air interface according to Fresnel's equations; only the
transmitted fraction reaches the Teflon, which returns it diffusely with a
reflectivity of 0.99, the value measured by Janecek and Moses for three or
more layers of tape. The polish is described with the UNIFIED model of
Geant4: the normal to the surface seen by each photon deviates from the
geometric normal by a Gaussian angle of $1.3^\circ$ rms, a value obtained for
crystals of the same finish when the optical simulation was calibrated.

The air gap has a consequence that dominates the light collection. A photon
whose direction is beyond the critical angle ($27.7^\circ$ in BGO,
$34^\circ$ in \csitl) on the face it meets is totally reflected, and since
the faces of the box are parallel, a photon beyond the critical angle on all
three pairs of faces stays trapped until the roughness of the surface,
Rayleigh scattering in the bulk or absorption changes its direction or ends
its life. The roughness is therefore not a detail: it sets how fast the
trapped light leaks towards the readout face.

\subsection{Coupling and window}

The sixth face is coupled through optical grease ($n = 1.42$) to the SiPM
tiles, whose photosensitive surface is covered by 0.15~mm of silicone resin
($n = 1.57$, from the datasheet). A photon leaving the crystal through this
face crosses two interfaces, crystal to grease and grease to resin, and at
each one it is reflected or transmitted according to Fresnel's equations. The
first interface dominates (from 2.15 or 1.79 down to 1.42); the second
reflects 0.3\% at normal incidence. A reflected photon returns to the crystal
and continues its life. The step from the resin into the silicon is not
needed, since the detection efficiency quoted by Hamamatsu includes it. The detection efficiency is
applied once the photon is inside the resin above an active area, as a
function of its wavelength and of the overvoltage
(Section~\ref{sec:sipm}), and independently of its angle of incidence. This
independence is an assumption: the datasheet measures the efficiency at
normal incidence from air, whereas our photons arrive from the grease at all
angles up to the critical one. It is listed in Section~\ref{sec:open}.

\subsection{The SiPM array}
\label{sec:layout}

The array is made of four Hamamatsu S14161-6050HS-04 tiles, each with
$4\times4$ channels of 6.0~mm active width on a 6.2~mm pitch. The tiles are
butted, and since the active area stops 0.2~mm short of the package edge, the
active areas of two channels across a tile boundary are 0.4~mm apart. With
the origin at the centre of the matrix, the 64 centres are at $\pm 3.2$,
$\pm 9.4$, $\pm 15.6$ and $\pm 21.8$~mm in each coordinate.

This geometry differs from an ideal array in three ways. The matrix is
50.0~mm wide, so the outer channels overhang the crystal by 1.0~mm and only
5.2~mm of their width faces it. Inside the $48 \times 48$~mm$^2$ footprint
the active area is 93.4\% of the total, the rest being the 0.2~mm gaps
between channels, the 0.4~mm seam that crosses the centre in both directions
and the overhang. And a photon transmitted into the resin above one of these
gaps meets the surface of the package between the channels, whose
reflectivity is not known; the model takes it diffuse with a reflectivity
of 0.5, and it is listed in Section~\ref{sec:open}.

The array enters the simulation as a list of active rectangles, each with
its channel number, so that the same code describes this array, an ideal one
or any future layout. The channel numbering is part of the configuration
of each run (Section~\ref{sec:formats}).

\section{Source and gamma transport}
\label{sec:gamma}

The \Na source is a point on the axis of the crystal, 30~mm from the centre
of the face opposite the SiPMs. In each decay it emits a gamma of
1274.5~keV and, in 90.3\% of the cases, a positron that annihilates into two
back-to-back gammas of 511~keV. The directions are isotropic. The simulation
generates whole decays, draws the directions of the 1275~keV gamma and of
the 511~keV pair, and transports the gammas whose line meets the front face.
The face covers 13\% of the sphere seen from the source, so the 1275~keV
gamma enters in 13\% of the decays; the 511~keV pair enters in 25\% of the
decays with a positron, twice as often, because either partner can be the
one aimed at the face, and never both. Keeping the gammas of one decay together
matters for the spectrum: when the 1275~keV gamma and a 511~keV gamma both
deposit energy in the same event, their energies add, which produces the sum
peak at 1786~keV, and the Compton continuum of the 1275~keV line lies under
the 511~keV photopeak with its real weight. The gammas arrive at angles of up
to $49^\circ$ at the corners of the face, so the depth of the interactions
differs from that of a beam at normal incidence.

Inside the crystal the gammas are followed by the transport code of
PTCrysp, which simulates photoelectric absorption and Compton scattering with
cross sections tabulated up to 10~MeV. Pair production, open above
1.022~MeV, is treated as a local absorption; at 1275~keV it is below 1\% of
the total cross section. Three effects are not simulated: the fluorescence
that follows a photoelectric absorption on the K shell of bismuth, whose
77~keV X-ray can escape when the interaction is within a fraction of a
millimetre of a surface and produces an escape peak at 434~keV; the Doppler
broadening of the Compton scattering; and the scattering of the gammas in the
source holder and in the walls of the cryostat. They are listed in
Section~\ref{sec:open}.

\section{Scintillation}
\label{sec:scintillation}

Each energy deposit emits a number of photons drawn from a Poisson
distribution whose mean is the light yield of the material times the energy
deposited (Table~\ref{tab:configs}). The crystal itself broadens the
photopeak beyond the photon statistics. The light yield per unit energy of a
scintillator depends on the energy of the electron that deposits it, and a
gamma of a given energy is converted into electrons of different energies
depending on how it interacts (one photoelectron, or a chain of Compton
electrons followed by a photoelectron), so the total light fluctuates from
event to event even for the same deposited energy. This intrinsic resolution
is known for \csitl, close to 5\% FWHM at 511~keV, and less well for BGO. The
non-proportionality curve of BGO has been measured (Gentile et al., 2015,
and the references therein): relative to 662~keV, the light per keV is 73\%
at 23~keV, 89\% at 60~keV, about 92\% between 80 and 120~keV, 93\% at
186~keV and 98.6\% at 356~keV. A 511~keV deposit that proceeds through
Compton electrons of 100 to 300~keV therefore yields a few percent less light
than a single photoelectron, which is the origin of an intrinsic term of the
same order as in \csitl. The simulation takes 5\% FWHM for both crystals,
as a parameter, and applies it as a Gaussian smearing of the light of each
event; the direct measurements that separate this term from the photon
statistics (Moszynski et al.) remain to be checked. A more faithful
treatment is possible, because the gamma transport records every deposit
with its energy: weighting the light of each deposit by the curve at the
energy of its electron produces the Compton-chain part of the intrinsic
resolution for every line and for the continuum, leaving only the
fluctuations within a single electron track to a smaller smearing. It is
planned as a refinement.

Each photon is emitted at a time after the deposit drawn from the decay
profile of the material: a single exponential for BGO, and for \csitl the
sum of a 730~ns component carrying 64\% of the light and a 3340~ns component
carrying the rest. The two components of \csitl matter for the electronics,
whose shaper is much shorter than either, because the stored signal follows
the rate of arrival of the photons at the beginning of the pulse, which the
fast component sets. For BGO at 250~K the decay time is the 0.84~\us of the model
of Gironnet et al., in a material description of its own, so that the
values of the calibration against the Ding cube stay with it.

Each photon is also given a wavelength drawn from the tabulated emission
spectrum of the material. The refractive index of the crystal, the Rayleigh
scattering length (which scales as $\lambda^{-4}$ from the calibrated value
at the emission peak) and the detection efficiency of the SiPM are then
evaluated at that wavelength. The bulk absorption length keeps its
calibrated scalar value.

\section{Optical transport}
\label{sec:transport}

The photons are transported one by one on the GPU, each with its own stream
of random numbers, so that a run is reproducible photon by photon whatever
the number of threads. A photon moves in straight lines inside the box; at
each step the code decides whether it is absorbed in the bulk or scattered by
Rayleigh scattering before reaching a face, and at the face it applies the
surface model described in Section~\ref{sec:geometry}: Fresnel reflection or
transmission with the roughened normal, the air gap and the Teflon on the
wrapped faces, the grease and the resin on the readout face. The photon ends
its life when it is absorbed in the bulk, in the wrapping, in the package
between the SiPMs or in a SiPM without producing a photoelectron, or when it
is detected. The code checks that every photon ends in exactly one of these
ways.

Three extensions of the transport are needed for the actual detector, and
all three have been described above: the array of active rectangles with the
reflecting surface between them, the second interface and the resin layer
(the lateral displacement of the photon inside 0.15~mm of resin is
negligible against the 6~mm pitch and is not tracked), and the wavelength of
each photon with the tabulated refractive index, Rayleigh length and
detection efficiency.

The optical simulation writes one file per run. For every event it records
the energy and position of each interaction of the gammas in the crystal,
with the number of interactions and the type of the first one, the number of
scintillation photons emitted and the number detected, and the detected
photoelectrons as a list of records of channel, detection time and
wavelength, stored consecutively for all events with an index giving where
each event starts. A record occupies 7 bytes, so a 511~keV event takes about
70~kB in \csitl and 15~kB in BGO, and a run of $10^5$ events per
configuration takes a few gigabytes of disk and a few minutes on the GPU.

\section{Response of the SiPM}
\label{sec:sipm}

The SiPM is the S14160-6050HS element of Hamamatsu (datasheet KAPD1064E,
November 2024); the S14161 tiles carry the same element. The model describes
each channel separately.

\paragraph{Breakdown voltage and overvoltage.} The breakdown voltage is
38.0~V at 25~$^\circ$C and rises by 34~mV per kelvin, so it is near 36.4~V at
250~K and 34.5~V at 196~K; a measurement on one of our channels at that temperature gave
34.3~V. Within a tile the channels differ by 0.1~V typically and 0.2~V at
most. The simulation draws the breakdown voltage of each channel from that
spread, or takes it from the configuration of the run. The overvoltage of a channel is its bias minus its
breakdown voltage, after the per-channel trim of the ASIC input DAC if it is
used.

\paragraph{Gain.} The charge of one avalanche is proportional to the
overvoltage: $0.93 \times 10^6$ electrons per volt, which gives
$2.5 \times 10^6$ at 2.7~V.

\paragraph{Detection efficiency.} The probability that a photon arriving on
the active area produces an avalanche is the product of a spectral factor,
read from the datasheet curve and normalised to one at its maximum of
450~nm, and the efficiency at 450~nm as a function of the overvoltage: 37\%
at 1.7~V, 50\% at 2.7~V, 54\% at 3.2~V and constant above, because the
probability of triggering an avalanche saturates.

\paragraph{Crosstalk and afterpulsing.} An avalanche emits a few photons
that can fire a neighbouring cell at the same time. The probability grows
linearly with the overvoltage, from 2.5\% at 1.6~V to 13\% at 3.7~V. The
model treats it as a branching process: every avalanche, primary or itself
produced by crosstalk, fires a Poisson number of neighbours of mean
$\lambda = -\ln(1-p)$, so that the probability of at least one extra
avalanche is the datasheet value $p$ and the total number of avalanches per
primary follows a Borel distribution of mean $1/(1-\lambda)$~\cite{vinogradov}.
Compared with a geometric number of the same $p$, the branching gives more
large groups: at 19.5\% (5~V) the probability of seven or more extra
avalanches is $3.5\times10^{-4}$ against $1.1\times10^{-5}$, which matters
for the rate of dark triggers at thresholds of several photoelectrons. Afterpulsing, the delayed release of a carrier trapped during
the avalanche, is only qualified as low in the datasheet; measurements on
devices of the same technology give about 1\% at 3~V, growing with the
square of the overvoltage, with delays of tens to hundreds of nanoseconds.
The model uses 1\% at 3~V with a time constant of 100~ns, as an adjustable
parameter, and reduces the charge of the afterpulse by the fraction of the
recharge of the cell completed at its delay, with the recovery time given
with the pulse shape below. Its effect is a correlated addition of about
one per cent to the charge of every channel.

\paragraph{Dark counts.} Thermal carriers start avalanches at a rate that
grows with the overvoltage like the detection efficiency and with the
temperature by a factor 2 every 9~K. The datasheet dark current at
25~$^\circ$C and 2.7~V, $2.5~\mu$A, corresponds to $6 \times 10^6$
avalanches per second per device once the crosstalk is taken out; at 196~K
and 6.5~V a measurement on this array gave 8 to 15~kHz per channel, and
the temperature law gives about 150~kHz per channel at 250~K. The model adds dark
avalanches, with their crosstalk and afterpulses, over the whole acquisition
window of every event. The electronics hold their output at a fixed level,
so the mean current of the dark avalanches gives no offset: the model
subtracts the mean dark charge before the shaping and keeps only its
fluctuations. Every record is preceded by a warm-up, four peaking times of
the longest shaper and ten fall times of the pulse, in which the dark
avalanches and the noise already run through the filters, so that the
response is stationary from the first sample of the record.

\paragraph{Pulse shape and cell occupancy.} An avalanche discharges its
cell, which recharges through its quenching resistor with the time constant
$\tau_{\rm rec} = R_q C_d$ of the resistor and the cell capacitance. The
recharge current flows into the input resistance $R_{\rm in}$ of the
electronics, in parallel with the capacitance of the whole device,
$C_t = 2000$~pF for the S14160-6050HS~\cite{hpk}, which filters it with the
time constant $R_{\rm in} C_t$~\cite{seifert}. The current in the input is
therefore the difference of two exponentials, rising with the shorter of the
two time constants and falling with the longer one; the fast component that
passes through the parasitic capacitance of the quenching resistor is
neglected. The datasheet does not give $\tau_{\rm rec}$. A fall of about
60~ns has been reported for this device, measured through an amplifier with
a pole-zero circuit that shortens the pulse~\cite{kampert}, and the 6~mm
devices of the series decay more slowly than the smaller ones because of
their larger quenching resistance~\cite{cattaneo}. The model takes
$\tau_{\rm rec} = 50$~ns, and $R_{\rm in} = 50~\Omega$ for the three
readouts: it is the termination prescribed by the Citiroc
datasheet~\cite{citiroc}, the Radioroc2 datasheet does not give the
impedance of its signal input, and the DT2745 is assumed in its
50~$\Omega$ single-ended version. Then $R_{\rm in} C_t = 100$~ns sets the
fall, and the pulse peaks 69~ns after the avalanche and is 176~ns wide at
half maximum. This is the slower of the plausible cases, and the
conservative one for the pile-up of dark pulses. Once $\tau_{\rm rec}$ is
known, the model computes the pulse at the input of each readout from its
input resistance. A device has 14\,331
cells, and a cell that has just fired needs $\tau_{\rm rec}$ to recover; the
model keeps track of this, although only the brightest \csitl channel comes
within a percent of being affected.

The output of this stage, for every channel, is a train of avalanches with
their times and charges, which is the input of the electronics.

\section{Electronics}
\label{sec:electronics}

\subsection{The Citiroc 1A}
\label{sec:citiroc}

Two chips read the 64 channels, 32 each, with the channels assigned by the
configuration of the run. The chain of each channel, as the datasheet (DS7004,
version 2.5) describes it and as the simulation reproduces it, is the
following.

\begin{enumerate}
\item {\bf Input.} The train of avalanches becomes a voltage across the
  termination of the input, which the datasheet specifies as 50~$\Omega$ in
  series with 100~nF; with the 2~nF of the device it sets the fall of the
  pulse to 100~ns (Section~\ref{sec:sipm}). The input DAC of the channel
  does not touch the signal; it shifts the bias of the SiPM and therefore
  enters the model through the overvoltage.
\item {\bf Preamplifiers.} Two voltage preamplifiers work in parallel on each
  channel: a high-gain one, with 15~pF of input capacitance and a gain
  adjustable between 10 and 600, and a low-gain one, with 1.5~pF and a gain
  between 1 and 60. The simulation gives each channel its own gain, drawn
  around the nominal value with an adjustable spread.
\item {\bf Shapers.} Each preamplifier feeds a CR-RC$^2$ shaper with a
  peaking time between 12.5 and 87.5~ns, set in steps of 12.5~ns for the
  whole chip, separately for the high- and the low-gain shaper, each of
  which also has its own hold signal. A CR-RC$^2$ shaper is one differentiating stage followed by
  two integrating ones, all with the same time constant $\theta = t_p/2$,
  from 6.25 to 43.75~ns over the available settings. The preamplifiers are
  AC-coupled voltage amplifiers~\cite{citiroc}: they amplify the voltage
  that the current of the SiPM develops across the 50~$\Omega$ termination,
  not its integral, so the differentiating stage acts on the current pulse
  itself. The impulse response of the shaper is then the derivative of the
  semi-Gaussian $(t/\theta)^2 e^{-t/\theta}$, and the shaped signal of an
  event is the convolution of the current of the avalanche train with it:
  a slow input is suppressed, and of the 100~ns pulse of our device the
  shaper keeps mostly the rising edge. Behind a charge-sensitive
  preamplifier, as on the Radioroc2, the differentiation cancels the
  integration of the preamplifier and the response to one avalanche is the
  semi-Gaussian itself; an earlier version of this model treated the
  Citiroc that way.
  The datasheet states the order of the shaper and nothing more; the exact
  response is a parameter to be checked against a single-photoelectron
  waveform if one can be probed on the test board.
\item {\bf Noise.} A Gaussian noise series, shaped by the chain, is added
  after the shaper. With 2~nF of device capacitance at the input the series
  noise of the preamplifier dominates; it grows in proportion to the
  capacitance at the input and falls as the inverse square root of the
  peaking time~\cite{spieler}. The datasheets give one point each: a
  signal-to-noise ratio of 10 for one photoelectron at a gain of $10^6$
  for the Citiroc, with neither the capacitance nor the shaping time
  stated, and a ratio above 10 for 160~fC with 100~pF at the input for the
  Radioroc2~\cite{radioroc}, that is at most 1.6~fC. Scaled to 2~nF, about
  twenty times more, the Radioroc2 figure becomes about 30~fC, or 0.07
  photoelectron at the gain of $2.8\times10^6$ of 3~V, and the Citiroc is
  assumed similar. The simulation takes 0.1 photoelectron rms on the
  high-gain path at the longest shaping of the chip, a conservative
  value, and scales it as $(87.5~\mathrm{ns}/t_p)^{1/2}$ at shorter
  peaking times; the low-gain path has the same noise in ADC counts, ten
  times more in photoelectrons.
\item {\bf Trigger.} A fast shaper with 15~ns peaking time, on the high-gain
  path, feeds two discriminators whose thresholds are common to the chip
  (10~bits) with a 4-bit trim per channel. One of them provides the trigger
  output of the channel; the other fills the hit register and the OR of the
  32 channels. The simulation records, for each channel, every time the
  fast-shaped signal crosses the threshold, the discriminator re-arming when
  the signal falls below it; the trigger of the acquisition is then a
  configurable function of these times (the OR of one chip, the OR of both,
  or the earliest time at which $N$ channels have crossed within a
  coincidence window), as the FPGA builds it in the real system.
\item {\bf Peak detector and hold.} When the chip triggers, each channel
  starts storing the maximum of its shaped signal, on the high-gain and the
  low-gain path separately, and keeps it until the hold arrives, a fixed
  delay after the trigger (up to 500~ns). Each of the 64
  channels stores its own maximum, and a channel that receives little light
  stores the maximum of its noise within the hold window; this gives a
  positive bias to the dim channels which the simulation reproduces because
  it stores the maxima the same way. At room temperature the maximum is
  set by the dark pulses and their crosstalk companions rather than by the
  electronic noise. The zero of the low-gain path, on which the energy is
  measured, is taken from self-triggered dark-count events as the mean
  low-gain value of the channels whose discriminator did not fire
  (Section~\ref{sec:comparison}), and subtracted channel by channel. It is
  not exactly an additive constant: in a channel that receives light the
  stored maximum is that of the signal, which a dark pulse changes only if
  it falls on top of it. The energy scale is therefore taken from the 511
  and 1275~keV peaks, which absorbs the remaining offset. The alternative
  mode of the chip, in
  which the signal is sampled at a fixed time instead of at its maximum, is
  available in the simulation as well.
\item {\bf Readout and conversion.} The stored values are read out through a
  multiplexer at up to 5~MHz and digitised by an external ADC. The simulation
  applies the gain and offset of the ADC, its quantisation (12~bits by
  default) and the saturation of the chain, which is reached either at
  400~pC of input charge or at the full scale of the ADC, whichever comes
  first on each path. A high gain at full scale is not a saturation of the
  measurement, since the energy is read on the low gain; a channel is
  flagged when its low gain saturates. The dead time of the readout is recorded but does not
  reduce the number of events, because the rates of the simulated source are low.
\end{enumerate}

The stored value of a channel is the peak of the shaped signal, calibrated
in photoelectrons by the peak that one avalanche produces. For a
scintillation pulse much longer than the peaking time the shaped signal
does not reach the amplitude that all the photoelectrons would give if they
arrived together: it is the convolution of the arrival rate with the pulse
of one avalanche and with the shaper response, and its maximum, divided by
the maximum of the same convolution for a single avalanche, is the fraction
of the photoelectrons that the measurement keeps. With the peaking time at
87.5~ns and the avalanche pulse of Section~\ref{sec:sipm} (peak at 69~ns,
100~ns fall), the fraction is 0.29 for BGO at room temperature, 0.10 for
\csitl, whose 730~ns component carries 64\% of the light, and 0.12 for BGO
at 250~K with its 0.84~\us decay. For a decay time $\tau$ much longer than
the peaking time the fraction is the ratio of an effective window to
$\tau$. Behind the voltage preamplifier the window is 115~ns for the pulse
of the model, against 280~ns behind a charge-sensitive one, because the
differentiation suppresses the slow part of the train while the single
avalanche, which serves as the unit, keeps its rising edge. The pulse at the
Citiroc input is therefore the parameter that fixes these fractions, and it
is listed in Section~\ref{sec:open}. The rise of the scintillation itself, below a
nanosecond in BGO and some tens of nanoseconds in \csitl, is short compared
with the window and changes nothing. The photon statistics of the energy
measurement is that of this fraction and not that of all the
photoelectrons; the simulation computes it with the 64 channels, their
noise and their dark pulses.

\subsection{The DT2745 digitizer}
\label{sec:digitizer}

The CAEN DT2745 samples its 64 channels every 8~ns with 16 bits over a
full scale of 4~V, so one count is 61~\textmu V, through an analog
bandwidth of 20~MHz and a gain programmable between 1 and 100 for each
group of 16 channels. Each SiPM is connected directly to the input of one
channel, 50~$\Omega$ single-ended in the version assumed here (100~$\Omega$
differential in the other); there is no preamplifier and no shaper. The
pulse of one avalanche is that of Section~\ref{sec:sipm}; the 20~MHz
bandwidth of the digitizer, an 18~ns rise, is short compared with its rise
and is neglected. Its area, the quantity the analysis measures, is 25~times
its peak in count-samples. The height of the peak is a parameter, 3.2~counts
per volt of overvoltage at gain~1, as an assumption: the pulse of the
model, carrying the charge of one avalanche into 50~$\Omega$, gives about
0.6~counts per volt, five times less (Section~\ref{sec:open}). Each channel fires when its sampled signal rises
above its baseline by a threshold in counts, and the acquisition takes an
event when $N$ channels fire within a coincidence window; it then stores,
for each of the 64~channels, a record of fixed length with a fixed number
of samples before the trigger. The analysis of a record estimates the
baseline of the channel as the mean of its pre-trigger samples and defines
its charge as the sum of the samples in a gate that starts at the trigger
and lasts long enough to contain the scintillation decay, minus the
baseline times the number of samples in the gate. The single-photoelectron
area, measured on dark pulses, converts that charge into photoelectrons.

The simulation of this readout does not build the waveforms. It computes,
from the train of avalanches of each channel, the charge that the analysis
would have extracted from the waveform, and writes it in the same event
file as the Citiroc charges. This is possible because every operation
between the avalanches and the charge is linear except the trigger. The
sampled waveform of a channel is the sum of the pulses of its avalanches,
each pulse scaled by the charge of its avalanche, plus the noise of the
digitizer. The charge is a weighted sum of samples: weight one in the gate,
weight minus the ratio of the gate length to the baseline length in the
baseline window, zero elsewhere. Applying that weighted sum to the pulse of
one avalanche gives a number that depends only on where the avalanche falls:
its full area if it falls inside the gate, a fraction of it if it straddles
an edge, minus the scaled area if it falls in the baseline window. The
charge of the event is therefore the sum over the avalanches of the channel,
scintillation and dark alike, of their charge times this number, plus the
weighted sum of the noise samples. For white noise with an rms $\sigma_s$
per sample the latter is a Gaussian with rms
$\sigma_s \sqrt{N_g + N_g^2/N_b}$, with $N_g$ and $N_b$ the numbers of
samples in the gate and in the baseline window, and one draw per channel
and event is all the noise the charge needs. In units of photoelectrons
this noise is 0.6 for a gate of 1~\us and 2 for a gate of 10~\us at 5~V of
overvoltage and gain~1; the low-frequency noise and the correlation
between the channels of one group observed with this digitizer are not in
this model, and can be added as a second Gaussian common to the 16 channels
of a group.

The trigger is the one non-linear step and the only one that needs the
shape of the pulse. The simulation evaluates the sampled amplitude of each
channel, the sum of the pulses of its avalanches on the 8~ns grid, around
the times where avalanches occur, and records the first sample at which it
exceeds the threshold. The trigger time of the event is then the time at
which $N$ of these crossings fall within the coincidence window, as in the
acquisition, and it is the origin from which the gate and the baseline
window are placed. The threshold decides which events are taken and, at
room temperature, how often the dark pulses take one instead. The
single dark photoelectron is not the problem: a dark avalanche fires
neighbouring cells with the crosstalk probability, 19\% at 5~V, so one dark
pulse in 700 carries four photoelectrons or more, and over the 64 channels
such pulses come about once per microsecond; a threshold of two or three
photoelectrons triggers on them in every window. The emulation gives the
efficiency for the photopeak and for the Compton events and the rate of
dark triggers as a function of the threshold and of the majority; the
threshold is the lowest whose dark-trigger rate stays below 1~kHz at 95\%
confidence. Its value depends on the pulse and on the crosstalk model and
is given, for each readout, in the study at room temperature.

What goes into the event file is then the same as for the Citiroc: one
charge per channel, as a 32-bit integer in count-samples, the set of
channels that crossed the threshold in place of the hit register, and the
trigger time. The single-photoelectron area and the noise per channel go
into the calibration group. The saturation of the input, at a peak
amplitude of 4~V divided by the gain, is checked on the brightest channel
and flagged; it is not reached in the configurations studied. A subsample
of events, a thousand per run, is also written as full waveforms, built with
the same pulses and noise, for figures; they are not used by the analysis.

The gate is a choice of the analysis that the simulation informs, in its
position as in its length. The emulation scanned both: opening the gate
earlier than 50~ns before the trigger brings nothing, because the trigger
fires within a few tens of nanoseconds of the first light, and every
additional nanosecond of gate collects dark pulses; at 5~V of overvoltage
the scan, made with an earlier and faster model of the pulse, gave a best
gate of 1~\us for BGO at room temperature and 3~\us for \csitl, and it is
to be repeated with the present one. The
study takes 5~V of overvoltage and gain~4 of the digitizer, where
one photoelectron is 64~counts against a nominal noise of 4~counts per
sample. The gain is the decisive choice for the calibration, not for the
energy: at room temperature each channel produces 4.4 million dark
counts per second at 5~V, 5.5 million fired cells with their crosstalk,
one every 180~ns on average, and the simulated waveforms show that at gain~1, with
16~counts per photoelectron, the one- and two-photoelectron peaks of the
dark-pulse spectrum are too shallow to fit channel by channel, whatever
the estimator of the amplitude. At gain~4 a matched filter (the samples
weighted with the pulse of one cell, after subtracting the mean of the
four preceding samples) gives clean peaks, with a width of 6\% of the
gain and a fitted gain that follows the true one to 0.1\% between
channels. The length of the gate is the other choice. The gate must contain the scintillation decay, but at room
temperature every channel produces dark counts at about 4.4~MHz, and in a
gate of 10~\us the 64~channels gather about 2800 of them, 3500 fired cells
with their crosstalk, against 1650 photoelectrons of signal in BGO: their
fluctuation alone is 4\% rms. A gate of 1~\us for
BGO at room temperature, whose decay is 300~ns, and of 4 to 5~\us for \csitl brings
that term below 1\%. At 250~K the dark rate is about 30 times lower
and the gate can take the whole 0.84~\us decay with no penalty: the
digitizer, with a gate of 4~\us, stores 0.99 of the photoelectrons of cold
BGO, where the Citiroc stores 0.12 of them (Section~\ref{sec:citiroc}) and
the Radioroc2, at its longest shaping, 0.84.

\subsection{The Radioroc2}
\label{sec:radioroc}

A single chip reads the 64 channels (datasheet version 2.0, January 2025).
Its chain has the structure of the Citiroc one, with the differences
listed below, and the simulation applies the same code with these
differences as settings.

\begin{enumerate}
\item {\bf Input.} The datasheet does not give the impedance of the signal
  input; its 100~$\Omega$ option belongs to the input DAC that sets the
  bias of the SiPM. The simulation takes 50~$\Omega$, as for the Citiroc,
  until the board is chosen.
\item {\bf Preamplifiers and shapers.} Two charge preamplifiers per channel,
  with gains between 5 and 80 on the high-gain path and 0.5 and 8 on the
  low-gain one, each followed by a shaper whose peaking time is set channel
  by channel: 20 to 300~ns in steps of 20~ns, or, in the slow mode, 120~ns
  to 1.8~\us in steps of 120~ns. The order of the shaper is not stated; the
  simulation takes CR-RC$^2$, as for the Citiroc, as an assumption. The slow mode is the natural choice for \csitl and
  for cold BGO, whose 0.84~\us decay through 1.8~\us of shaping stores 0.84
  of the photoelectrons (Section~\ref{sec:citiroc}); at room temperature
  a long shaping also collects more dark pulses, about 14 photoelectrons
  per channel in the effective window of the longest setting, so that the
  shaping time has an optimum per crystal which the emulation gives.
\item {\bf Noise.} As for the Citiroc: the datasheet bound, at most 1.6~fC
  with 100~pF at the input, gives about 0.07 photoelectron once scaled to
  the 2~nF of our device, with no shaping time attached. The simulation
  takes the conservative 0.1 photoelectron rms at 100~ns, scaled as
  $(100~\mathrm{ns}/t_p)^{1/2}$; the dark pulses, which the model generates
  explicitly, grow instead with the peaking time.
\item {\bf Triggers.} Each channel has a time preamplifier of 200~MHz
  bandwidth with two discriminators, T1 and T2, of independent thresholds
  common to the chip with a per-channel trim, and a charge discriminator TQ
  on the output of the low-gain shaper. The time preamplifier sees the
  avalanche almost unshaped, so T1 and T2 respond to the instantaneous
  amplitude within a few tens of nanoseconds: for BGO at room temperature the brightest
  channel of a 511~keV event reaches only 4 to 5 photoelectrons there,
  while the dark pulses with crosstalk reach the same once per microsecond
  over the array, and a single time threshold cannot separate them. The
  charge trigger sees the integrated light of the event, hundreds of
  photoelectron-equivalents for the photopeak against a handful for a dark
  pulse, and is the trigger the simulation takes by default for the slow
  scintillators; T1 and T2, single channel or in a majority built by the
  FPGA of the board, are emulated as an alternative. The pulse at the time
  preamplifier is not the one measured through the 20~MHz digitizer; the
  simulation takes a 5~ns rise and a 20~ns fall as parameters, which only
  enter the T1 and T2 numbers.
\item {\bf Acquisition trigger, hold and peak detectors.} The peak detectors
  of all channels are opened by one acquisition trigger, selectable among the
  chip-wide OR of T1, of T2 or of TQ, the local trigger of each channel, or
  an external signal; the hold follows it after a delay set on the chip
  between 2~ns and 2.6~\us, or comes from outside. Each path stores the
  maximum of its shaped signal from the trigger to the hold, as in the
  Citiroc, with the same positive bias of the dim channels.
\item {\bf Readout and conversion.} The stored values of the two paths
  leave through two analog multiplexers at 200~ns per channel, 12.8~\us
  for the 64 channels, to the ADC of the acquisition board, which the
  simulation takes as the 12-bit converter of the evaluation board. The
  low-gain path covers 2000 photoelectrons at a gain of $10^6$, about
  320~pC, at its default preamplifier gain of 0.53; the high-gain path,
  whose default gain is 5.3, saturates ten times lower. The ceilings scale
  inversely with the preamplifier gains, but the high gain cannot be set
  below 5, so its ceiling cannot exceed about 76 avalanches at our SiPM
  gain of $2.8 \times 10^6$, which the brightest channel of a 511~keV
  event in BGO at room temperature exceeds in a quarter to a half of the events; the
  chip is therefore operated as designed, both paths read and combined,
  the high gain for every channel below its ceiling and the low gain above
  it, and the simulation stores that dual-gain value. Each channel can
  instead route its shaped signal to a pin of its own, for a parallel
  digitisation that the simulation does not use.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Files}
\label{sec:formats}

\subsection{The photoelectron file}

The optical simulation writes its photoelectron records in one HDF5 file per
run. The records of all events are stored consecutively in three arrays,
\code{channel} (one byte), \code{t\_ns} (a 32-bit float) and
\code{lambda\_nm} (16 bits), and an array \code{offset} of length one more
than the number of events gives the position of the first record of each
event. The event index is the same as in the file of interactions and photon
counts written by the same run. The attributes of the file record the
configuration of the run and the version of the code.

\section{Observables}
\label{sec:comparison}

The analysis extracts the performance from the event file of each
configuration and readout, using only what the readout records, and
compares it with the quantities of the group \code{/mc}.

\begin{enumerate}
\item {\bf Single-photoelectron spectra.} Built from dark pulses, channel by
  channel, each readout with its own data only, they give the gain of each
  channel and its spread, as a system with many crystals would have to
  calibrate itself with its own readout. For the two chips every crossing
  of the discriminator of a channel at half a photoelectron holds the high
  gain of that channel at the maximum of its pulse, the hold delay being
  the one that maximises the held value; the shaping is short enough for
  the dark pulses not to pile up under the held value. The 1, 2 and 3
  photoelectron peaks of the spectrum, the higher ones from crosstalk, give
  the gain as the slope of the straight line through their positions and
  the zero of the high gain as its intercept. The zero of the low-gain path
  is the mean low-gain value of the channels whose discriminator did not
  fire, in self-triggered dark-count events taken with the settings of the
  run, and its gain is the high-gain one times the ratio of the two paths.
  For the digitizer a matched filter on the waveform gives the
  one-photoelectron peak, the baseline being subtracted. The fitted gains
  are compared channel by channel with the gains the simulation used.
\item {\bf The two paths of the chips.} On both chips the high-gain path
  gives the single-photoelectron peaks and the low-gain path the energy.
  The peaks are usable only at a short shaping of the high gain, where the
  dark pulses do not pile up under the held value. With the pulse and
  noise of the model, the Radioroc2 calibrates every channel at 50 and
  100~ns (the fitted gains scatter by 3.6\% between channels at 100~ns, the
  setting adopted) but not at 300~ns or at 25~ns, where the noise hides
  the peaks; the Citiroc calibrates every channel at 25, 50 and 87.5~ns,
  with a scatter of 2.2, 5.1 and 7.5\%, and its high gain is set to 25~ns
  while the low gain stays at 87.5~ns for the energy. The gain of the low-gain path follows
  from the ratio of the two paths at their two peaking times, which the
  charge-injection input of each chip measures (the Citiroc has one on
  each preamplifier, the Radioroc2 a 1.5~pF capacitor per channel). The
  simulation assumes this ratio known. The injected charge arrives at once,
  while the charge of an avalanche is spread over the 100~ns of its pulse,
  so that at a short shaping the high gain responds less to an avalanche
  than to the same injected charge; the ratio measured by injection has to
  be corrected for this with the pulse shape. The 1275 to 511 ratio of the
  low-gain sum then tests the linearity of that path alone.
\item {\bf Pedestals and noise.} The rms of the pedestal of each channel
  and of the sum, which enter the energy resolution.
\item {\bf The photoelectron budget.} For the photopeak at 511~keV: the
  scintillation photons emitted; the fraction that reaches the active area
  of a channel and the fraction of those detected, whose product is the
  light efficiency; the photoelectrons per keV, which is the absolute
  response. Each readout then records, in units of the single-photoelectron
  gain, a charge that contains the crosstalk and the afterpulses of these
  photoelectrons and the dark counts in its window, and that the two ASICs
  reduce to the fraction of the pulse their shaping keeps. The budget goes
  back from this calibrated charge to the photoelectrons: the mean of the
  dark counts is subtracted, the charge is divided by the stored fraction,
  and the result by the mean number of fired cells per photoelectron, which
  for branching crosstalk of probability $p$ is $1/(1-p)$, times the
  factor of the afterpulses. The number obtained is the absolute response
  measured by the readout, and it is compared with the number of
  photoelectrons in \code{/mc}. The ratio of the photoelectrons between the
  BGO configurations at room temperature and at 250~K gives the change of
  the light yield with the temperature.
\item {\bf The energy spectrum} of the equalised sum of the 64 channels: the
  511 and 1275~keV lines, the sum peak, the Compton continua and the
  backscatter region. The ratio of the two photopeak positions, 2.5 if the
  chain is linear, tests the dynamic range and the trigger; for the
  peak-detector chips the energy scale is the straight line through the two
  peaks, since the pedestal of the peak detectors leaves an offset. The
  width of the 511~keV line is decomposed into the noise of the pedestals,
  the statistics of the stored photoelectrons, the fluctuation of the
  crosstalk, the afterpulses and the dark counts, and the intrinsic
  resolution put into the simulation.
\item {\bf The spatial resolution.} The position of the interaction is
  reconstructed from the 64 charges of the event, with the barycentre of
  the light and with the neural network developed for the idealised readout,
  and compared with the position in \code{/mc}. The reconstruction on the
  output of each readout is not done yet (Section~\ref{sec:open}).
\item {\bf The brightest channel.} The distribution of the largest charge in
  an event, and its ratio to the total, describe the light distribution near
  the readout face; in \csitl its tail shows the saturation of the chain.
\item {\bf Channel maps.} The fraction of events in which each channel is hit
  and the mean charge it collects show the response of the outer channels
  (which see only 5.2~mm of crystal), of the central seam and of the tile
  boundaries. The reflectivity of the surface between the channels and the
  roughness of the crystal are the two parameters that change these maps.
\item {\bf The seed-channel map.} The position of the 511~keV photopeak for
  the events grouped by their brightest channel, in units of the median over
  the channels: a map of the non-uniformity of the light collection.
\item {\bf Dependence on the overvoltage}: light, crosstalk, resolution and
  saturated fraction against the overvoltage, with the curves of
  Section~\ref{sec:sipm}.
\item {\bf The readouts on the same crystal.} The photoelectrons at
  511~keV recorded by the digitizer, which integrates the whole decay, and
  by the Citiroc or the Radioroc2, which store a fraction of them, are in
  the ratio computed in Section~\ref{sec:citiroc} from the pulse shape and
  the shaping time: 0.29 for BGO and 0.10 for \csitl with the Citiroc, 0.97
  for BGO and 0.71 for \csitl with the Radioroc2 at 1.8~\us, at room
  temperature. The ratio follows from the shaping chains alone, with no
  free parameter.
\item {\bf Trigger efficiency and gate length.} The photopeak content and
  the rate of dark triggers against the threshold, for each readout, and
  for the digitizer the resolution against the length of the gate; these
  curves fix the settings of each readout.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Validation}
\label{sec:validation}

The code is tested at each of its extensions, with tests that have an
analytic answer or reduce a new feature to an old one.

\begin{itemize}
\item The array described as active rectangles reproduces, with the
  rectangles of an ideal array, the result of the ideal array; with the
  gaps and no reflection on the surface between the channels, the fraction
  of photons lost under uniform illumination equals the geometric dead
  fraction.
\item When the grease and the resin have the same index the second
  interface reflects nothing; when the resin has the index of the crystal
  and there is no grease, the result is that of a crystal in optical contact
  with the sensor.
\item A spectrum reduced to a single wavelength reproduces the monochromatic
  simulation.
\item The photoelectron records, binned in time, reproduce the time-binned
  maps written by the run.
\item The rate of gammas on the front face equals the solid-angle fraction,
  and the distribution of their incidence angles the analytic one.
\item A single avalanche through the shaper gives the analytic CR-RC$^2$ peak
  at $t_p$; a Poisson train with a long decay time gives the stored fraction
  of Section~\ref{sec:electronics} within its statistics; a peak detector
  fed with noise only gives the expected bias of the maximum of a Gaussian
  series; the saturation clips at the configured charge.
\item The pulse of one avalanche peaks at $\tau_a\tau_b\ln(\tau_a/\tau_b)/
  (\tau_a-\tau_b)$ and falls with the longer constant; the branching
  crosstalk gives no extra avalanche with probability $1-p$ and the Borel
  probabilities for the larger groups; the average of the shaped dark
  counts is zero once their mean charge is subtracted, from the first
  sample of the record; a single avalanche held at its maximum after its
  own crossing gives the pedestal plus one gain; a chain behind a voltage
  preamplifier passes no constant input.
\item The energy resolution of a simulated configuration agrees with the
  quadrature of its stated terms.
\end{itemize}

\section{Implementation}
\label{sec:implementation}

The optical simulation is CryspLight, a Julia package whose transport
kernel runs on the GPU of Apple computers through Metal and, identically, on
the CPU. Its reference version of the transport and its GPU version are kept
equal draw by draw, and a test proves that they produce the same result bit
for bit on the CPU; every extension described here is written in both.

The state of the code before this work is kept under the git tag
\code{pedsim}, and the extensions grow on the branch \code{fullsim}. Nothing
in the existing behaviour is changed: each extension is switched on by a
block in the configuration file of the run and switched off by its absence,
in which case the code follows the old path with the same random numbers. A
test runs a small configuration of the old kind after every change and
compares its output with stored checksums. The existing configurations,
output files and analysis scripts are left as they are; the new simulation
has its own configurations under \code{fullsim\_runs/}, grouped by
study, its own analysis under \code{fullsim\_analysis/} and
its documents under \code{docs\_fullsim/}. The analysis is written in Julia,
a library of readers, fits, spectra and maps and one script per study; the
scripts compute and write files, HDF5 for arrays and CSV for tables, under
\code{fullsim\_analysis/results/}, and Python scripts draw the figures from
those files, so that a figure is remade without recomputing and every
number cited in a document is in a file produced by a tracked script. The SiPM and electronics
simulation is a new module, \code{src/frontend/}. Simulation outputs go to
the directory \code{output/}, which is not under version control.

The optical stage records every photon that reaches the resin above an
active area, with the detection efficiency set to one in the transport, and
the SiPM stage applies the efficiency by keeping each record with the
probability of Section~\ref{sec:sipm} for its wavelength and the overvoltage
of the run. Thinning a Poisson process this way is exactly equivalent to
applying the efficiency photon by photon during the transport, and it lets
one optical run serve every overvoltage of a scan. The photoelectron file is
written in chunks of decays as the run proceeds, so that a run of any length
streams to disk; the random streams of the decays and of their photons are
disjoint between chunks, and the gamma-level content of a run does not
depend on how it was chunked.

The GPU imposes a few constraints on the extensions: the tables of the
spectra and of the wavelength-dependent quantities travel in one buffer of
32-bit floats, the switches and scalars in one structure, and no new random
number is drawn unless the corresponding extension is on.

\section{Open items}
\label{sec:open}

\begin{itemize}
\item Whether the detection efficiency depends on the angle of incidence
  when the photons arrive from the grease rather than from air.
\item The reflectivity and the specularity of the surface of the package
  between the channels.
\item The light yield and decay time of BGO at 250~K, taken from Gironnet
  et al.: the decay time may be 20 to 30\% long, and the yield changes by
  about 1\% per kelvin near 250~K, so that the temperature of the crystal
  must be known to a few kelvin.
\item The intrinsic resolution of BGO.
\item For the Citiroc: the exact response of the shaper, the noise
  spectrum, the droop of the peak detector during the readout, and the
  pulse shape at the input of the preamplifier with a device of 2~nF.
\item The recovery time of the cells and the input resistance of each
  readout, which fix the pulse, and the peak height per photoelectron of
  the digitizer.
\item For the Radioroc2: the order of the shaper, the noise, the impedance
  of its signal input with 2~nF, and the acquisition board and
  ADC to be used. Its dark-count calibration spectrum at room temperature
  shows a population at zero, crossings of the time discriminator with no
  signal on the high gain, which is absent at 250~K and is not understood.
\item For the digitizer: the noise below 1~MHz and its correlation within
  a group of 16 channels, and whether the gain stage lowers the noise per
  photoelectron, which decides which gain serves the cold configuration,
  where the overvoltage may be lower.
\item In the gamma transport: fluorescence and escape of the K-shell X-rays,
  Doppler broadening, and scattering in the source holder and in the
  cryostat.
\item The temperature of the SiPMs relative to the crystal in the cold
  configuration, which sets their breakdown voltage and dark rate.
\item The reconstruction of the position on the output of each readout,
  done so far only on the idealised readout.
\item The photoelectron budget of Section~\ref{sec:comparison}, with the
  crosstalk, the afterpulses and the dark counts taken out, for every
  configuration and readout.
\end{itemize}
