\section{Purpose}

The simulation of the detector describes the electronic noise of the Citiroc front end with a
simple model, and the first pedestal measurements on the bench do not follow it. This note
records what the model assumes, what was measured, where the two disagree, the hypotheses that
could explain the disagreement and the measurements that would distinguish them. It is a
working record: it will be updated as the measurements arrive.

\section{The model}

Each channel of the Citiroc amplifies the SiPM signal with a preamplifier and shapes it with a
slow shaper (one differentiation and two integrations, CR-RC$^2$, with a peaking time $t_p$
that can be set to 25, 50 or 87.5~ns) in two parallel paths, the high gain and the low gain.
In the peak stretch mode used throughout, a peak detector follows the maximum of the shaped
signal between the trigger and the hold, which comes \texttt{HoldDelay} after the trigger, and
the value it holds is digitised by a 13-bit ADC.

The simulation makes three assumptions about the noise:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Series white noise.} The noise is that of the preamplifier input, white, and
  after the slow shaper its rms scales as $t_p^{-1/2}$. It is set to 0.1~photoelectron (pe)
  in the high gain at 87.5~ns and 3~V of overvoltage, hence 0.06~pe at 5~V; at 50 and 25~ns
  it is 1.32 and 1.87 times larger.
\item \textbf{Same noise in both gains.} The low gain has the same rms in ADC counts as the
  high gain at the same shaping.
\item \textbf{Independent channels.} The noise of each channel is independent of that of the
  others.
\end{enumerate}
In the peak stretch the stored value is the maximum of the noise over the window, so the
pedestal rises and narrows as \texttt{HoldDelay} grows. For white noise shaped at 25~ns, a
window of 96~ns already contains several independent fluctuations: the model predicts a rise
of 1.7 times the rms of a single sample at 96~ns and 2.2 at 296~ns, with the width reduced to
0.6 and 0.5 of that rms.

\section{The measurements}

The data were taken on 22 October 2026 with five A5202 boards (two Citirocs each, 320
channels), the SiPMs connected but biased at 36.5~V, below breakdown, so that no avalanche
contributes, and a periodic trigger every 1~ms, uncorrelated with any signal. Each run has
about 10\,000 events. Three runs at \texttt{HoldDelay} = 0 change the shaping of the high gain
(25, 50, 87.5~ns); five more at 25~ns change \texttt{HoldDelay} from 96 to 496~ns; a last run
repeats the first. The low gain is shaped at 87.5~ns in all runs. Channels 0--31 of two boards
are not read (a readout problem under investigation), which leaves eight chips of 32 channels.

All the results are in ADC counts. Converting them to photoelectrons needs the gain of the
high gain in counts per pe, which these runs, without avalanches, cannot give.

\section{Separating the common mode}\label{sec:method}

Part of the noise is shared by all the channels of a chip, and it can be separated because the
32 channels of a Citiroc are read at the same trigger. The steps are illustrated on one chip
that works normally (board 2, channels 32--63, run 26, high gain at 25~ns).

\paragraph{1. Deviations from the pedestal.} For channel $i$ in event $e$, with $x_i(e)$ the
value read,
\begin{equation*}
d_i(e) = x_i(e) - \operatorname{median}_e\, x_i .
\end{equation*}
Besides the fluctuations of each channel, the map of $d_i$ shows vertical stripes, events in
which most channels are above or below their pedestal together (Figure~\ref{fig:stepA}).

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{noise_steps_A.png}
\caption{Board 2, channels 32--63, run 26: deviation of each channel from its pedestal in 100
consecutive events (below), and the mean of the 32 deviations in each event (above).}
\label{fig:stepA}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{2. Jumps of the whole chip.} The number of channels far from their pedestal,
\begin{equation*}
N(e) = \#\{\, i : |d_i(e)| > 60 \text{ counts} \,\},
\end{equation*}
with 60 counts five times the rms, separates a different phenomenon: on one board, in
0.1--0.4\% of the events, all the channels jump together by 60--230 counts
(Figure~\ref{fig:stepB}). Normal events have $N \le 6$ and the jumps $N = 24$--32; events
with $N \ge 10$ are set aside. This is a fault of that board, not part of the noise.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{noise_steps_B.png}
\caption{Left: board 0, channels 32--63, run 26, around a jump (colour saturated at $\pm40$
counts). Right: distribution of $N(e)$ on board 0 and on the example chip, with the cut.}
\label{fig:stepB}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{3. Common mode.} The common mode of an event is the mean of the deviations,
\begin{equation*}
\mathrm{CM}(e) = \frac{1}{32}\sum_{i=1}^{32} d_i(e) .
\end{equation*}
Its rms is 6.2 counts on the example chip (Figure~\ref{fig:stepC}, left). The separation
assumes that every channel receives the same common mode, which is checked with the slope of
each channel against it,
\begin{equation*}
b_i = \frac{\mathrm{cov}_e(d_i, \mathrm{CM})}{\mathrm{var}_e\, \mathrm{CM}} .
\end{equation*}
It is between 0.85 and 1.2 (median 1.04) for all the channels of all chips but 15, which take
much less and are discussed in Section~\ref{sec:sipm}.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{noise_steps_C.png}
\caption{Left: common mode of the example chip. Centre: deviation of channel 40 against the
common mode, with the fitted slope. Right: slopes $b_i$ of all channels in the runs with
HoldDelay 0; the channels below 0.75 do not see their SiPM.}
\label{fig:stepC}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{4. Total, coherent and incoherent noise.}
\begin{align*}
\sigma_\mathrm{tot} &= \operatorname{median}_i\, \mathrm{rms}_e\, d_i , \\
\sigma_\mathrm{inc} &= \operatorname{median}_i\, \mathrm{rms}_e \big(d_i - \mathrm{CM}\big)\,\sqrt{32/31} , \\
\sigma_\mathrm{coh}^2 &= \mathrm{var}_e\, \mathrm{CM} - \sigma_\mathrm{inc}^2/32 .
\end{align*}
The two corrections come from the mean itself: subtracting it removes from each channel 1/32 of
its own noise, and it keeps $1/\sqrt{32}$ of the incoherent noise of the channels, which is not
common. On the example chip $\sigma_\mathrm{tot} = 11.9$, $\sigma_\mathrm{coh} = 5.9$ and
$\sigma_\mathrm{inc} = 10.4$ counts, and $\sigma_\mathrm{tot}^2 = \sigma_\mathrm{coh}^2 +
\sigma_\mathrm{inc}^2$ within 0.1\%. Subtracting the common mode narrows the distribution of
the deviations from 12.0 to 10.2 counts (Figure~\ref{fig:stepD}). In what follows, the values
are medians over the chips of a run.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.55\textwidth]{noise_steps_D.png}
\caption{Example chip: deviations of all channels in all events, before and after subtracting
the common mode of each event.}
\label{fig:stepD}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{5. Correlation between chips.} The common modes of the two chips of a board are
correlated by 0.6--0.85 in all boards and runs, those of different boards are not correlated
(below 0.12; Figure~\ref{fig:stepE}).

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{noise_steps_E.png}
\caption{Common mode of chip 0 against chip 1 of board 2 (left) and of board 2 against board 3
(right), run 26.}
\label{fig:stepE}
\end{figure}

\section{Results}

\subsection{Noise against shaping}

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{noise_vs_shaping.png}
\caption{Pedestal noise of the high gain at \texttt{HoldDelay} = 0 against the shaping time:
total, coherent and incoherent rms (median over the eight chips, bars from the lowest to the
highest chip), and the low gain at 87.5~ns. Left, in ADC counts, with the model scaled to the
incoherent noise at 87.5~ns; right, divided by the value at 87.5~ns, with the model ratio.}
\label{fig:shaping}
\end{figure}

The total noise of the high gain is 12.3, 12.6 and 13.1 counts at 25, 50 and 87.5~ns: it
barely changes, and it is slightly larger at the longest shaping, while the model predicts a
noise 1.87 times larger at 25~ns than at 87.5~ns (Figure~\ref{fig:shaping}). The two parts
behave differently. The incoherent noise, 10.5, 9.8 and 9.3 counts, does fall when the shaping
is shortened, but by 1.13 and 1.05 instead of 1.87 and 1.32. The coherent noise, 5.9, 7.7 and
8.2 counts, grows with the shaping, which is the behaviour of a noise whose power is
concentrated at low frequencies. Its share of the variance goes from a quarter at 25~ns to
40\% at 87.5~ns, so the third assumption of the model fails as well.

The coherent noise belongs to the board: as shown in step 5 of
Section~\ref{sec:method}, the common modes of the two chips of a board are correlated, the
more so at the longest shaping (0.8 at 87.5~ns), and those of different boards are not.

\subsection{Low gain against high gain}

At 87.5~ns, where both paths have the same shaping, the low gain has a total noise of 5.8
counts against 13.1 for the high gain, 0.45 times as much, while the model assumes the same
noise. The reduction is not the same for the two parts: the incoherent noise of the low gain
is 5.5 counts (0.59 of the high gain), its coherent noise only 1.9 (0.23).

\subsection{Pedestal against HoldDelay}

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{noise_vs_holddelay.png}
\caption{High gain at 25~ns against \texttt{HoldDelay}. Left, rise of the pedestal from its
value at 0~ns, in units of the total rms at 0~ns (median over the channels of each board).
Right, total, coherent and incoherent rms divided by their value at 0~ns. In black, the model:
the maximum of white noise shaped at 25~ns over a window of \texttt{HoldDelay}.}
\label{fig:hold}
\end{figure}

The pedestal rises and narrows with the window, as expected, but slowly and steadily
(Figure~\ref{fig:hold}). At 96~ns it has risen by 0.2 rms against 1.7 in the model, at 296~ns
by 0.5--0.6 against 2.2, and at 496~ns by 0.8--0.9 against 2.4, and it has not yet stopped
rising. The width falls to 0.87 of its initial value at 96~ns and to 0.58 at 496~ns, where the
model reaches 0.6 already at 96~ns and stays at about 0.5. The coherent and incoherent parts
narrow in the same way. The data therefore behave as if the noise kept its value for a few
hundred nanoseconds, much longer than the shaping time, so that a longer window adds new
fluctuations only slowly.

\subsection{The common mode enters through the SiPM}
\label{sec:sipm}

Fifteen channels take much less common mode than the others, with slopes $b_i$ of 0.02--0.6
in all runs against more than 0.85 for every other channel (Figure~\ref{fig:slopes}). On
board 0 they are channels 15, 55, 57, 59, 61 and 63, exactly the six channels that showed no
avalanches in the threshold scans taken with the SiPMs above breakdown, whose SiPM is
connected but whose signal is lost in a bad contact or in the connector. The other nine are
on boards 1, 3 and 4, where no threshold scan has been taken yet. A channel that does not see
its SiPM does not receive the common mode, so the common mode reaches the preamplifiers through
the SiPMs. The pedestal rms alone does not separate these channels: a SiPM at the input raises
the noise of its preamplifier, but at 87.5~ns the six channels sit at 0.82--0.87 of the median
rms of their board and other channels at 0.81--0.89.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{bench_slopes.png}
\caption{Slope of each channel against the common mode of its chip, median over the runs with
HoldDelay 0 (bars from the lowest to the highest run), with a cut at 0.75. In red, the six
channels of board 0 without avalanches in the threshold scans.}
\label{fig:slopes}
\end{figure}

\section{Hypotheses}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A low-frequency noise dominates.} After a CR-RC shaper, white series noise
  scales as $t_p^{-1/2}$, white parallel noise (the leakage current and the bias resistors) as
  $t_p^{+1/2}$, and a $1/f$ noise does not depend on $t_p$. A mixture in which the $1/f$ or
  parallel components dominate gives a noise nearly independent of the shaping, and a noise
  with long correlation times explains the slow rise of the pedestal with the window. The two
  observations would then have one cause.
\item \textbf{The coherent noise comes from the bias of the SiPMs.} Each board has its own HV
  module, which biases the 64 SiPMs through a common line; below breakdown each SiPM is a
  capacitor that couples any ripple of that line to the input of its preamplifier. This gives
  a noise common to the board, uncorrelated between boards and concentrated at low
  frequencies, as observed, and it is the only hypothesis that explains why channels without
  their SiPM do not receive the common mode (Section~\ref{sec:sipm}). The reference voltages
  or the ground of the board would reach every channel, connected or not. A pick-up on the
  cables or adapter boards between the SiPMs and the board would also enter through the
  SiPMs and is not excluded.
\item \textbf{The peak detector is not ideal.} If the peak detector follows the signal with a
  finite speed, or loses part of the held value during the window, it acts as a further
  filter and the rise with the window is slower than for an ideal maximum. This would affect
  the response to real pulses as well.
\item \textbf{The low gain has its own noise.} The two paths share the input but have their own
  preamplifiers, and the coherent part, which enters before them, is reduced by the ratio of
  their gains, while the incoherent part is reduced less. A model with the same noise in
  counts in both gains is then too simple.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Consequences for the simulation}

For a single channel the measured noise is 10--13 counts in the high gain and about 6 in the
low gain, and its size in photoelectrons is not yet known. For the sum of the 64 channels,
which gives the energy, the coherent part matters most: the incoherent noise adds in
quadrature and grows as $\sqrt{64} = 8$ times that of one channel, while the coherent noise
adds linearly and grows 64 times. With the numbers of the high gain at 25~ns, about 85 counts
come from the incoherent noise and about 350 from the coherent one (with the measured
correlation of 0.6--0.85 between the two chips of a board). The simulation needs a
noise model with a correlated component per board, and with a dependence on the shaping and
on the window taken from the data.

\section{What is needed}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The gain in counts per pe}, at 25, 50 and 87.5~ns, to express the noise in
  photoelectrons and compare with the 0.06--0.11~pe of the model. It comes from the
  hold-delay scans with the SiPMs at 5~V of overvoltage (bench note 2), on the plateau where
  the single-pe peaks are resolved, and from the calibration with the periodic trigger
  (bench note 3).
\item \textbf{The response of the peak detector to a known pulse}, from the same hold-delay
  scans: the delay after the trigger at which the stored amplitude reaches its plateau,
  compared with the 31, 58 and 90~ns of the model (plus the latency of the board), and whether the plateau stays flat at long delays. This tests the
  third hypothesis and shows that the shaping time actually changes.
\item \textbf{A pedestal run with the HV off}, at 25 and 87.5~ns. The SiPMs stay connected,
  so a pick-up on the cables would remain, while a ripple of the bias line would disappear
  with the coherent noise. This decides the origin of the coherent noise (second
  hypothesis).
\item \textbf{The analog probe of the slow shaper on the oscilloscope}, with the SiPMs below
  breakdown: the noise waveform gives its correlation time and its spectrum directly, and
  decides between the first and the third hypotheses.
\item \textbf{A fit of the model to both data sets}, on our side: series, parallel and $1/f$
  components, plus a coherent component per board, adjusted to the noise against shaping and
  to the pedestal against the window at the same time.
\end{enumerate}
\noindent The first two come from the next bench session (bench note 2); the third and fourth
are short additions to be scheduled after it.
