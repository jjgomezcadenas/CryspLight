\section{Purpose}

The electronic noise of the readout enters the energy resolution, the width of the
single-photoelectron peaks used for the calibration, and the value stored by channels that
receive little light. The simulation describes it with a simple model, which has to be checked
and, where it fails, replaced by one that reproduces the measurements. This note describes how
the noise of the system ---the SiPMs, the Citiroc and the A5202 board that carries it--- is
measured, then applies the method to the measurements as they arrive, records where they
disagree with the model, the hypotheses that could explain the disagreement and the
measurements that would distinguish them. It is a working record: new measurements are added
as cases, and the last section states what is stable enough to be passed to the model of the
simulation.

\section{The readout chain}

Each A5202 board reads 64 SiPMs with two Citiroc chips of 32 channels, and biases them with its
own HV module through a common line. In each channel of the Citiroc the SiPM signal is
amplified by two preamplifiers in parallel, the high gain and the low gain, and each path is
shaped by a slow shaper (one differentiation and two integrations, CR-RC$^2$, with a peaking
time $t_p$ that can be set to 25, 50 or 87.5~ns). In the peak stretch mode a peak detector
follows the maximum of the shaped signal between the trigger and the hold, which comes
\texttt{HoldDelay} after the trigger, and the value it holds is digitised by a 13-bit ADC. The
32 channels of a chip are held and read at the same trigger.

\section{How the noise is measured}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Pedestal runs}

The noise is measured in pedestal runs: the SiPMs are connected and biased 1--2~V below
breakdown, so that no avalanche contributes while their capacitance stays at the input of the
preamplifiers, and the board is triggered periodically, uncorrelated with any signal. Each run
gives, for every channel and event, the value stored in both gains. Changing the shaping
between runs, with \texttt{HoldDelay} = 0, gives the dependence of the noise on the peaking
time, which separates its components (Section~\ref{sec:observables}); changing
\texttt{HoldDelay} at a fixed shaping changes the window over which the peak detector takes the
maximum, which tells how fast the noise varies. A last run repeating the first checks the
stability of the pedestals.

\subsection{Separating the common mode}
\label{sec:cm}

Part of the noise may be shared by all the channels of a chip, and it can be separated because
they are read at the same trigger. For one chip and one run, with $x_i(e)$ the value of
channel $i$ in event $e$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Deviations from the pedestal.} The pedestal of channel $i$, $p_i$, is the median
  of $x_i$ over the events of the run, which the jumps and outliers found in the next steps
  cannot drag. The deviation from it is
  \begin{equation*}
  d_i(e) = x_i(e) - p_i .
  \end{equation*}
\item \textbf{Outliers of a channel.} A channel may depart from the rest of its chip in a
  single event, a fault of that channel rather than noise. In each event the reference of the
  chip, $r(e)$, is the median of the deviations of its 32 channels in that event, which one
  channel cannot move. The pull of channel $i$ in event $e$ is
  \begin{equation*}
  z_i(e) = \frac{d_i(e) - r(e)}{s_i} ,
  \end{equation*}
  where $s_i$ is the noise of the channel, measured with the median absolute deviation (MAD).
  The MAD of a set of values is obtained in two steps: first the median of
  the values, then the median of the absolute distances of the values to it, so that half of the
  values lie within one MAD of the median. For a Gaussian of width $\sigma$ half of the values
  lie within $0.674\,\sigma$ of the centre, so $\mathrm{MAD} = 0.674\,\sigma$ and the width is
  estimated as $1.4826\,\mathrm{MAD}$. Unlike the rms, the MAD is not changed by a few extreme
  values, which is what a cut on extreme values needs. Here $s_i = 1.4826\,\mathrm{MAD}(d_i - r)$, over
  the events. The channel is an outlier in that event if $|z_i(e)| > 5$. With Gaussian noise a
  channel passes $5 s_i$ with a probability of $6\times10^{-7}$ per event, about 0.2 times in
  a run of 32 channels and 10\,000 events. The outliers are left out of the common mode and of
  the widths, and counted for each channel: a channel that is often an outlier is faulty.
\item \textbf{Common mode.} The common mode of an event is the mean of the deviations of the
  channels that are not outliers,
  \begin{equation*}
  \mathrm{CM}(e) = \frac{1}{n(e)}\sum_{i \,\notin\, \text{outliers}} d_i(e) ,
  \end{equation*}
  with $n(e)$ their number, 32 in almost every event.
\item \textbf{Is there a common mode?} CM varies from event to event even if the channels are
  independent, with an rms equal to that of one channel divided by $\sqrt{32}$. The reference
  is made from the data themselves by shuffling: in the table of the deviations, with one row
  per event and one column per channel, the values of each column are put in an independent
  random order. Each channel keeps its values, and so its pedestal, rms and distribution, but
  the values in a row no longer come from the same trigger, so nothing common can move them
  together. A common mode shows as an rms of CM above that of the shuffled table, and as a
  correlation between the channels that the shuffling removes. For a common part that every
  channel takes in full, the correlation between any two channels is the fraction of the
  variance it carries, $\sigma_\mathrm{coh}^2/\sigma_\mathrm{tot}^2$ (step 7).
\item \textbf{Jumps of the chip.} If the whole chip moves in an event, the common mode of that
  event falls out of its own distribution. With $c$ the centre of that distribution over the
  run (its median) and $\sigma_\mathrm{CM} = 1.4826\,\mathrm{MAD}(\mathrm{CM})$ its width,
  measured as $s_i$ in step 2, an event is a jump if
  \begin{equation*}
  |\mathrm{CM}(e) - c| > 5\, \sigma_\mathrm{CM} ,
  \end{equation*}
  which a Gaussian common mode does with a probability of $6\times10^{-7}$ per event. The jumps
  are counted and the events set aside.
\item \textbf{Coupling of each channel.} The separation assumes that every channel receives the
  same common mode. This is checked with the slope of each channel against it,
  \begin{equation*}
  b_i = \frac{\mathrm{cov}(d_i, \mathrm{CM})}{\mathrm{var}(\mathrm{CM})} ,
  \end{equation*}
  with the covariance and the variance taken over the events. The slope
  is 1 for a channel that takes the full common mode. A channel with a slope far from 1
  is coupled differently from the others, and the slope says how.
\item \textbf{Total, coherent and incoherent noise.}
  Let $\sigma_i$ be the rms of $d_i$ over the events and $\sigma'_i$ that of $d_i - \mathrm{CM}$.
  The total noise of the chip, $\sigma_\mathrm{tot}$, is the median of the $\sigma_i$ over its
  channels; the incoherent noise, $\sigma_\mathrm{inc}$, is the median of the $\sigma'_i$
  multiplied by $\sqrt{32/31}$; and the coherent noise follows from the variance of the common
  mode over the events,
  \begin{equation*}
  \sigma_\mathrm{coh}^2 = \mathrm{var}(\mathrm{CM}) - \sigma_\mathrm{inc}^2/32 .
  \end{equation*}
  The two corrections come from the mean itself: subtracting it removes from each channel 1/32
  of its own noise, and it keeps $1/\sqrt{32}$ of the incoherent noise of the channels, which
  is not common. The check is $\sigma_\mathrm{tot}^2 = \sigma_\mathrm{coh}^2 +
  \sigma_\mathrm{inc}^2$.
\item \textbf{Correlation between chips.} The correlation coefficient of the common modes of
  two chips, over the events both read, says whether the common mode is that of the chip, of
  the board, or of the whole system.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{What each observable tells}
\label{sec:observables}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Noise against shaping.} After a CR-RC shaper, white series noise (the noise of
  the preamplifier input, which grows with the capacitance at the input) scales as
  $t_p^{-1/2}$, white parallel noise (leakage current, bias resistors) as $t_p^{+1/2}$, and a
  $1/f$ noise does not depend on $t_p$. The dependence of each part, coherent and incoherent,
  on the shaping points to its components.
\item \textbf{Pedestal against the window.} In the peak stretch the stored value is the maximum
  of the noise over the window, so the pedestal rises and narrows as \texttt{HoldDelay} grows.
  How fast it does so depends on how many independent fluctuations fit in the window, that is
  on the correlation time of the noise; an ideal peak detector is assumed.
\item \textbf{Low gain against high gain.} The two paths share the input and have their own
  preamplifiers: a noise entering before them is scaled by the ratio of their gains, a noise
  of their own is not.
\item \textbf{Coherent noise, its correlation and the slopes.} Where the common noise enters:
  a chip, a board or the system, and through which part of the input.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{From ADC counts to photoelectrons}

All the quantities above are in ADC counts. Expressing them in photoelectrons, which is what
the simulation and the calibration need, takes the gain of each path in counts per
photoelectron, at each shaping and overvoltage. It is measured with the SiPMs above breakdown,
from the spacing of the single-photoelectron peaks of the dark counts (hold-delay scan,
calibration with the periodic trigger); the measurement of 27 October is in
Section~\ref{sec:pe}.

\section{The noise model of the simulation}
\label{sec:model}

The simulation makes three assumptions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Series white noise.} After the slow shaper the noise rms scales as $t_p^{-1/2}$.
  It is set to 0.1~photoelectron (pe) in the high gain at 87.5~ns and 3~V of overvoltage,
  hence 0.06~pe at 5~V; at 50 and 25~ns it is 1.32 and 1.87 times larger.
\item \textbf{Same noise in both gains.} The low gain has the same rms in ADC counts as the
  high gain at the same shaping.
\item \textbf{Independent channels.} The noise of each channel is independent of that of the
  others.
\end{enumerate}
With an ideal peak detector, white noise shaped at 25~ns gives a pedestal that rises by 1.7
times the rms of a single sample at a \texttt{HoldDelay} of 96~ns and 2.2 at 296~ns, with the
width reduced to 0.6 and 0.5 of that rms: a window of 96~ns already contains several
independent fluctuations.

noise. The reduction is not the same for the two parts: the incoherent noise of the low gain
is 5.5 counts (0.59 of the high gain), its coherent noise only 1.6 (0.20).

The pedestal rises and narrows with the window, as expected, but slowly and steadily

\label{sec:peak}

The hold-delay scans of 27 October (menu~2) measure what the peak detector stores for a single
avalanche against the length of the window: the trigger limited to one channel, the position
of its 1-pe peak against \texttt{HoldDelay}.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{\dayb/hold_scan_response.png}

\begin{itemize}
\item At 25~ns the 1-pe peak reaches its plateau 32--40~ns after the trigger, where the model

  model at 58~ns.
\item Once the window contains the maximum, the stored amplitude stays constant to the end of
  the scan (248 or 496~ns): the peak detector holds a pulse as an ideal one.
\item The slow rise of the pedestal with the window (Section~\ref{sec:case2}) is therefore not
  a loss of the held value; a shift of the baseline with the hold delay remains as the
  candidate.
\end{itemize}

\section{Hypotheses}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A low-frequency noise dominates.} The coherent part grows with the shaping and
  the incoherent part barely falls: neither is series white noise. Still the only reading of
  the dependence on the shaping; the correlation time of the noise is not yet measured
  directly.
\item \textbf{The coherent noise enters through the SiPMs.} Confirmed in its mechanism: the
  blind channels take none of it, and the six channels of board~0 that regained their SiPM with
  the CAEN adapter take it in full. Its source is not identified: the noise is the same with
  the two adapter boards, so a pick-up on the adapter is excluded; the bias line, the cable or
  the matrix remain.
\item \textbf{The peak detector is not ideal.} Not supported by the response to a pulse
  (Section~\ref{sec:peak}). What remains unexplained is the pedestal, which rises with the
  window less than the maximum of the noise would, by board-dependent amounts; a shift of the
  baseline with the hold delay would do it.
\item \textbf{The low gain has its own noise.} Unchanged: 0.46 of the high-gain noise in
  counts, with the coherent part reduced more than the incoherent one.
\end{enumerate}

\section{What is needed}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A pedestal run with the HV off}, at 25 and 87.5~ns: a ripple of the bias line
  disappears with the coherent noise, a pick-up on the cables does not.
\item \textbf{The analog probe of the slow shaper on the oscilloscope}, SiPMs below
  breakdown: the correlation time and the spectrum of the noise, which decide hypothesis~1 and
  what the pedestal does on top of it.
\item \textbf{A fit of the model to the data}, on our side: series, parallel and $1/f$
  components plus a coherent component per board, adjusted to the noise against shaping and
  to the pedestal against the window at the same time.
\end{enumerate}
